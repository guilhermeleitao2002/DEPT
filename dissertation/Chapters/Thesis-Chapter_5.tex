\label{chap:evaluation}

This chapter evaluates DEPT against the threat model of \Cref{sec:sol-threat}, on the
corpus of \Cref{sec:sol-pipeline}. After the research questions, the setup and the
metrics, it reports how well and how early DEPT detects manipulated flows, how much a
gradient-guided attacker must spend to evade the ensemble, how much detection
purification gives back to the protected \ac{NIDS}, and what the full pipeline achieves
end to end. The answers to the research questions and the limitations these results
show are left to \Cref{chap:conclusion}.

% #############################################################################
\section{Research Questions}\label{sec:eval-rqs}

The evaluation is organized around two research questions, each tied to one of the
work objectives of \Cref{sec:intro-objectives}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1 (ensemble robustness).} Does heterogeneous stacking, a
    non-differentiable Random Forest fused with a differentiable Temporal Convolutional
    Network, measurably raise the cost of a transferable, gradient-guided evasion attack
    relative to a single-architecture baseline?
    \item \textbf{RQ2 (purification effectiveness).} Does the reconstructor move a
    flagged adversarial flow back onto the benign manifold, closing the detection gap
    seen by the protected NIDS, while emitting a realizable, protocol-compliant packet
    sequence?
\end{itemize}

\Cref{sec:eval-base} first reports the detection results, on which both questions
build. TANTGen cannot answer RQ1, because it takes its gradients from its own surrogate
and never from DEPT, so RQ1 uses the second adversary of \Cref{sec:sol-threat}, which
differentiates the \ac{TCN} directly (\Cref{sec:eval-gradablation}). RQ2 is answered on the reconstructor's test
pairs (\Cref{sec:eval-purification}) and end to end, through the \ac{NIDS} each attack
was built to evade, where DEPT's purifier is also compared with a published one
(\Cref{sec:eval-e2e}). Whether the full pipeline meets a real-time latency budget is not
evaluated in this work (\Cref{sec:concl-future}).

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:eval-setup}

The corpus has ten benign sources and thirty adversarial source--mode combinations,
with $8{,}624{,}958$ flows and $61{,}516{,}406$ packets in total. The models are trained,
validated and tested on the flow-level, source-stratified $70/15/15$ split of
\Cref{sec:sol-pipeline}. The CIC-DDoS-2019 floods supply most of the adversarial flows,
the SYN flood alone more than half, so the test set is mostly adversarial: of its
$1{,}293{,}737$ flows, $1{,}082{,}586$ ($83.7\%$) are adversarial and $211{,}151$ are
benign. For this reason the models are compared with AUROC, which does not depend on
the base rate. The Random Forest gives its first verdict at packet~$2$, so it does not
score the $6{,}179$ single-packet test flows.

The reconstructor learns from pairs of an adversarial flow and its clean original. Of
the $7{,}217{,}272$ adversarial flows, $2{,}566{,}489$ have such a counterpart;
$1{,}796{,}542$ pairs are used for training and $384{,}963$ are held out for testing.

All experiments ran on one server with two Intel Xeon Gold~5320 CPUs ($52$ cores),
$160$\,GB of RAM and two NVIDIA RTX~6000~Ada GPUs, using the software of
\Cref{sec:sol-tools}. Generating the thirty adversarial captures with TANTGen took
$26.7$\,h and tensorizing the corpus $11.2$\,h. Training took $2.7$\,h for the Random
Forest, including its grid search, $24.7$\,h for the \ac{TCN}, $3.4$\,h for the
meta-learner and $10$\,h for the reconstructor.

\section{Metrics}\label{sec:eval-metrics}

\paragraph{Detection.} A defense that must act before a flow ends cannot be judged only
at the end of the flow. The main detection metric is therefore \emph{AUROC@$t$}, the
ROC~AUC computed with each flow's prediction after its first $t$ packets, which shows how
soon a model becomes useful; AUROC@\textsc{max} uses the prediction at the last packet.
Two more metrics use the fixed threshold of $0.5$: the \emph{\acf{EDP}}, the median
packet at which a flow's score first crosses the threshold, and the \emph{detection
rate}, the fraction of adversarial flows that cross it at some packet.

\paragraph{Purification.} The \emph{reconstruction \ac{MSE}} compares a flow with its
true clean counterpart, before and after purification, on the feature dimensions
TANTGen perturbed. Since nPrint cells take the values $-1$, $0$ and $1$, a flipped bit
costs $1$ and a field that appears or disappears costs $4$, so values above $1$ are
possible. The main purification metrics are measured through an \emph{independent}
\ac{NIDS}, the detector each attack was built to evade (\Cref{subsec:eval-e2e-detect}),
so that no DEPT component grades itself. They are the \ac{ASR}, one minus the detection
rate of that \ac{NIDS}, and the \emph{restoration ratio}, the fraction of the detection
lost to TANTGen that purification recovers. The \emph{purification gain}, the drop in
the score of DEPT's own \ac{TCN} from the adversarial to the purified flow, is only a
sanity check, since that detector is part of DEPT. \emph{Protocol compliance}
is the fraction of realized packets that \texttt{tshark} parses without a
malformed-packet error.

The Random Forest alone is the best model from packet~$2$ on. Its AUROC is $0.9805$ at
its first checkpoint and $0.9813$ at the end of the flow, against $0.9099$ to $0.9334$
for the meta-learner and $0.8225$ to $0.8386$ for the \ac{TCN}. The design expected the
meta-learner to be at least as good as its best base learner, and it is not. It is the
best model only at packet~$1$, where the forest has no verdict yet ($0.8373$ against
$0.8191$ for the \ac{TCN}), and from packet~$2$ on it stays $8.7$ to $9.5$ points above
the \ac{TCN}. Its main contribution is robustness: under the gradient-guided attacker of
\Cref{sec:eval-gradablation}, the fused stack is harder to evade than either base learner
at low budgets.

At the $0.5$ threshold the meta-learner is conservative. It flags only $1{,}115$ of the
$211{,}151$ benign test flows, for a precision of $0.9985$ on the adversarial class, and
its \ac{EDP} is the first packet.

On some sources the meta-learner falls far below the forest
(\Cref{tab:eval-persource}). On \texttt{ntp\_ddos} \texttt{both} and \texttt{iat} it
reaches $0.653$ to $0.654$ where the forest reaches $0.999$, and on \texttt{ssdp\_ddos} it
reaches $0.927$ against $1.000$. A likely cause is the one anticipated in
\Cref{subsec:sol-meta}. The forest's logit enters the fusion token as a single feature,
updated only at checkpoints and missing before packet~$2$, while the \ac{TCN}'s logit
changes at every packet, so attention gives the forest too little weight. The auxiliary
``beat the best base'' loss helps only in part. It falls from $0.0267$ to $0.0177$ during
training, so the meta-learner does move towards its better base learner, and the
validation AUROC@$10$ rises from $0.9197$ to $0.9333$, mostly in the first epochs, but
the forest stays ahead.

Most combinations are solved: $21$ of the $30$ reach at least $0.99$ with the Random
Forest, and $13$ with the meta-learner. The weak spots are of four kinds.

Timing-only edits are hard for the \ac{TCN} and easy for the forest. On
\texttt{portscan\_iat} the \ac{TCN} reaches $0.652$ and the forest $1.000$, on
\texttt{syn\_ddos\_iat} $0.647$ and $1.000$, and on \texttt{ddos\_iat} $0.924$ and
$0.999$. The forest's timing statistics capture a change in timing that the \ac{TCN}'s
per-packet view misses, which is the kind of difference between learners that the
ensemble was built on.

The \ac{TCN} fails on the four reflection floods. It stays between $0.494$ and $0.552$
in every mode of \texttt{dns\_ddos}, \texttt{ldap\_ddos}, \texttt{ntp\_ddos} and
\texttt{ssdp\_ddos}, and on \texttt{ldap\_ddos}, the largest of them, it gives almost
exactly $0.500$ in all three modes. These floods are one- and two-packet UDP
exchanges, and a receptive field of $29$ packets has nothing to convolve over.

The \texttt{size} variants of the same four floods are at or below chance for every
model: $0.213$ to $0.476$ for the forest, $0.443$ to $0.497$ for the meta-learner and
$0.494$ to $0.531$ for the \ac{TCN}. Below chance means that, on these variants,
TANTGen's flows look more like generic benign traffic than the flood's own unedited
flows do. \Cref{sec:eval-purification} shows that on three of them the edit did not
change the flows at all.

Finally, \texttt{mirai} is the only source that no model solves in any mode, with a best
value of $0.926$, but no model falls below chance on it either.

All results so far come from one set of models trained on the ten sources together.
Detectors in the literature are often trained per attack instead, so the four model
stages were also run once per attack, with the same architectures, grid searches and
split protocol. \Cref{tab:eval-perattack} compares each specialist meta-learner with the
global one.

The two are indistinguishable on $13$ of the $30$ combinations, all of them already at
or near $1.000$. The specialists are better on timing edits: \texttt{ntp\_ddos} gains
$0.346$ and $0.347$ on \texttt{both} and \texttt{iat}, \texttt{syn\_ddos\_iat} $0.194$,
\texttt{ssdp\_ddos} $0.073$ on both modes and \texttt{portscan\_iat} $0.066$. A model
trained on one attack can learn that source's own timing, instead of mixing it with
nine other benign sources. The global model is better on \texttt{mirai} \texttt{both}
and \texttt{iat} (by $0.035$ and $0.033$) and on the \texttt{size} variants of the
reflection floods. There, however, both are below chance, and the global model is only
less wrong ($0.443$ against $0.192$ on \texttt{dns\_ddos}).

The specialists also show that two problems of the global model do not come from
training on all sources at once. The meta-learner again falls below its own forest, most
clearly on \texttt{mirai} ($0.705$ against $0.889$) and on \texttt{portscan} ($0.939$
against $0.999$). The per-attack TCNs also fail on the reflection floods, at $0.500$ to
$0.531$, while doing well on sources with longer flows ($0.990$ on \texttt{ddos} and
$1.000$ on \texttt{ssdp\_flood}).

A global model is still the better default, because a specialist can only be chosen by
a router that first names the attack, which is the classification problem the detector
is there to solve. Specialists are worth considering only for a known flood whose
evasion is in the timing.

\section{Robustness under a Gradient-guided Attacker (RQ1)}\label{sec:eval-gradablation}

% NOTE: numbers in this section come from
% data/models/gradient_ablation/gradient_ablation_metrics.json
% (scripts/gradient_ablation/run_gradient_ablation.py), full-scale run of 2026-07-20:
% 9,000 flows = 300 per source x 30 sources, 6 budgets, policy fpr_0.05, best-of
% (PGD u random) attacker under the monotone envelope.

The detection results above show that the ensemble detects TANTGen's manipulations,
but not what it is worth against an attacker who can differentiate one of its members.
This section answers RQ1 with the second adversary of \Cref{sec:sol-threat}.

\subsection{Attack Setup}\label{subsec:eval-gradablation-setup}

The attacker has white-box access to the \ac{TCN} and none to the Random Forest. It
crafts one perturbation against the \ac{TCN} with \ac{PGD}~\cite{madry2018towards}, and
the same perturbation is then given, unchanged, to the \ac{TCN}, the Random Forest and
the fused stack. Optimizing it again for each target would no longer measure transfer.
The perturbation adds padding and delay to each packet, on top of TANTGen's, and never
shortens a packet or makes it longer than the \ac{MTU}. The padding bytes are random.
The \ac{TCN} does not see payload bytes, so its gradient cannot choose them, but their
content changes the payload entropy that the forest reads. The budget is set per packet,
from $8$ bytes and $10\,\mu$s to $1{,}460$ bytes and $100$\,ms.

A defense tested only with a weak attack can look more robust than it
is~\cite{carlini2019evaluating,tramer2020adaptive}, so two other searches check the
\ac{PGD} attacker. A random search uses the same constraints, budget and number of
queries, but no gradient. AutoPGD~\cite{croce2020reliable}, from the \ac{ART}
library~\cite{nicolae2018adversarial}, checks that \ac{PGD} is not badly tuned: on a
relaxed version of the problem (sixteen \texttt{brute\_force} flows, without integer or
budget limits), it and both of our searches evade the \ac{TCN} on every flow. The
reported attacker takes, for each flow, the better of \ac{PGD} and random search.

The attack runs on $9{,}000$ test flows, $300$ per source--mode combination. Each
target's threshold is set at a $5\%$ false-positive rate on benign traffic, because the
three models are calibrated very differently: their median verdict on benign traffic is
$0.043$ for the forest, $0.379$ for the \ac{TCN} and $0.002$ for the stack, and the
matched thresholds are $0.4535$, $0.9820$ and $0.3658$. The \ac{ASR} is counted only over
the flows a target detects without perturbation, since a flow that is not detected cannot
be evaded. An attacker with a larger budget may also spend less, so the success rate at a
budget includes the flows evaded at every smaller budget.

\Cref{tab:eval-gradablation} and \Cref{fig:eval-evasioncost} answer RQ1. At $8$, $32$
and $128$ bytes per packet, the stack concedes $1.7\%$, $4.7\%$ and $8.5\%$ attack
success, against $8.5\%$, $10.7\%$ and $13.1\%$ for the forest and $40.5\%$, $50.1\%$
and $52.2\%$ for the \ac{TCN}. Over the whole grid, $74.5\%$ of the flows never evade the stack, against
$64.0\%$ for the forest and $45.5\%$ for the \ac{TCN}. The perturbation that breaks the
\ac{TCN} transfers poorly to the forest, and the stack is harder still, although not at
every budget: at $512$ bytes the stack ($18.9\%$) and the forest ($18.4\%$) are level,
and at $1{,}460$ bytes the stack is ahead again ($25.5\%$ against $36.0\%$). For
comparison, TANTGen's own padding averages $29$ to $32$ bytes per packet on
\texttt{portscan\_both} and \texttt{mirai\_size}, but $246$ to $289$ bytes on the
\texttt{size} variants of \texttt{ddos}, \texttt{ssdp\_flood} and \texttt{portscan},
closer to the $512$-byte point.

\paragraph{The meta-learner leans on the forest under attack.}
If the ensemble works as intended, the meta-learner should rely more on the forest when
the \ac{TCN} is attacked. To check this, each base-learner slot of the fusion token is given
back its clean value, and the change in the stack's verdict is measured. The share of
that change due to the forest is $0$ without attack, since there is nothing to
compensate, $0.529$ as soon as the attacker spends anything, and between $0.54$ and
$0.56$ at the larger budgets.

Against the \ac{TCN} itself, random search does better than \ac{PGD} at every budget
($40.1\%$ against $37.2\%$ at $8$ bytes, $52.8\%$ against $50.7\%$ at $1{,}460$). Neither
wins on every flow, so the best-of attacker is stronger than both ($40.5\%$ and $54.5\%$
at the same budgets). In practice, the attacker measured here is a search attack, and
the gradient adds little to it. The transfer result still holds, since both searches run
on the \ac{TCN}.

Two more limits apply to this result. The \ac{TCN}'s numbers rest on $3{,}374$ of the
$9{,}000$ flows, because at its threshold it detects at most $2\%$ of the unperturbed
flows on four sources (\texttt{brute\_force}, \texttt{ldap\_ddos}, \texttt{ntp\_ddos}
and \texttt{ssdp\_ddos}) and $9\%$ on \texttt{mirai}. And on the \texttt{both} and
\texttt{size} modes of \texttt{ddos} and \texttt{portscan}, and on
\texttt{ssdp\_flood\_size}, neither the forest nor the stack is evaded at any budget,
which only shows that padding and delay cannot overcome that much model confidence.

of its packets can be realized, so the \ac{NIDS} cannot score it.

nothing to undo: TANTGen barely moved the features, and the footprint is close to zero
before purification. On the \texttt{size} variants of \texttt{ldap\_ddos},
\texttt{ntp\_ddos} and \texttt{ssdp\_ddos} the edit did not happen at all. Every
adversarial tensor is identical to its clean counterpart, so there is no footprint to
measure, and detection does not change.

falls from $0.986$ to $0.762$ on \texttt{both}, from $0.993$ to $0.756$ on \texttt{iat}

because it cannot tell this source's adversarial and clean flows apart.
\texttt{mirai} is where purification works least well: on its \texttt{size} variant
the footprint only falls from $1.569$ to $0.973$, and detection
drops to about its level on the clean flows, because TANTGen's padding had made this
traffic easier for Kitsune to detect.

leaves out $450{,}758$ packets of \texttt{ssdp\_flood} \texttt{both} and \texttt{size}. A

realized faithfully and are reported apart. Among the realizable packets, compliance
is below $0.99$ only on \texttt{ddos} ($0.84$) and on \texttt{syn\_ddos\_iat}

change timing. The reconstructor predicts the original length, realization trims the
added bytes, and every packet leaves with $44$~bytes again. All twelve packets of the
three captures pass \texttt{tshark} without errors.

\section{End-to-End Evaluation}\label{sec:eval-e2e}

\Cref{sec:eval-purification} purified every test pair. This section first measures DEPT
as it would be deployed, with only the flows flagged by the meta-learner purified and
complete streams given to the protected \ac{NIDS}. It then compares DEPT's purifier with
a published one, on the test pairs of \Cref{sec:eval-purification}. Since the two parts
use different sets of flows, their numbers should only be compared within each part.

\subsection{Detect-then-Purify Pipeline}\label{subsec:eval-e2e-detect}

Here the full gated pipeline of \Cref{sec:sol-overview} is placed in front of the
protected \ac{NIDS}: the meta-learner flags flows, only the flagged flows are purified,
and the rest pass unchanged. Each attack is scored through the \ac{NIDS} it was built to
evade, the same model TANTGen calibrated its evasion against. This is Kitsune
(KitNET~+~AfterImage) for the two Kitsune sources, and TANTGen's ML-NIDS, its per-attack
CICFlowMeter flow classifiers, for the eight CIC-IDS2017 and CIC-DDoS-2019 attacks. For
every attack, three full streams are realized and scored: the \emph{clean} attack, which
shows the \ac{NIDS} working as intended, the \emph{adversarial} traffic before DEPT, and
the \emph{defended} traffic after DEPT. The three streams share the same packets and
differ only in sizes and timings (\Cref{subsec:sol-realization}). Full streams are used
because Kitsune, being partly volumetric, needs complete traffic. \Cref{tab:eval-baseline}
and \Cref{fig:eval-baseline} give the results.

Over $2{,}704{,}816$ scored flows, DEPT lowers the attack success from $21.1\%$ to

had removed. This average mixes very different outcomes. The gain is large where
TANTGen evaded: $38$ points on \texttt{ddos} and $32$ on \texttt{portscan}, and on single
modes $67.4$ points on \texttt{ddos\_size}, $67.0$ on \texttt{portscan\_both}, $59.7$ on

edits that purification undid in \Cref{sec:eval-purification}. On \texttt{ldap\_ddos},

evaded, since the adversarial traffic was already detected at $0.98$ to $1.00$, and the

on \texttt{brute\_force}. On other combinations the defense makes things worse:
\texttt{dns\_ddos} loses $22.5$ points on \texttt{both} and $22.7$ on \texttt{iat},
\texttt{portscan\_iat} $18.2$, \texttt{ddos\_iat} $12.2$ and \texttt{mirai\_size}
$23.9$. On \texttt{mirai\_size}, TANTGen's padding had raised Kitsune's detection from
$0.003$ to $0.242$, and by removing the padding DEPT also removed that gain.

The gate does not prevent these losses. It flags almost all flood traffic, except on the
\texttt{size} variants of the four reflection floods, where it flags almost nothing
($0.0\%$ on \texttt{ssdp\_ddos}, $0.2\%$ on \texttt{ntp\_ddos}, $0.8\%$ on
\texttt{ldap\_ddos} and $0.9\%$ on \texttt{dns\_ddos}), the same blind spot as in
\Cref{tab:eval-persource}. Flows that the \ac{NIDS} was still catching are therefore
purified too, and purifying every flow gives almost the same detection as the gated
pipeline ($0.8831$ against $0.8836$). The gate predicts whether a flow is adversarial,
while the pipeline needs to know whether purifying it will help, and the results above
show that these are different questions.

Because full streams are used, the defended streams also contain flows that the gate
and the purifier saw in training. On the flows that neither saw in training, selected as
in \Cref{subsec:eval-sota}, the gated pipeline lowers the attack success from $31.4\%$
to $17.7\%$, so the training overlap does not inflate the result.

\subsection{Comparison with a Published Purifier}\label{subsec:eval-sota}

To compare DEPT with the state of the art, its purifier is replaced by the diffusion-based
purifier of Merzouk et al.~\cite{merzouk2024diffusion}, the only network purifier of
\Cref{sec:rel-inference} with public code, while the flows, the realization step and the
protected \ac{NIDS} stay the same. The baseline keeps the authors' model and settings,
learns only from clean traffic, and its amount of noise is chosen as in the original, on
validation flows. Both purifiers are scored on the reconstructor's held-out test pairs
(\Cref{tab:eval-sota} and \Cref{fig:eval-sota}). Overall, the attack success is $31.5\%$
without defense, $18.9\%$ with DEPT and $40.3\%$ with the diffusion purifier, and the order
is the same on test flows whose original flow appears in no training pair.

The comparison favours DEPT in two ways. First, DEPT learns from matched TANTGen and
clean flows, while the baseline only sees clean traffic, and both are tested against
TANTGen, the attacker DEPT was trained on. Second, the baseline was designed for
flow-level features and had to be adapted to DEPT's per-packet sizes and timings; the
timing encoding chosen for it explains part of its losses on the floods whose detection
depends on timing.
