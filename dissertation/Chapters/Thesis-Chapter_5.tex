This chapter evaluates DEPT against the threat model of \Cref{sec:sol-threat}: a
black-box, protocol-compliant adversary, concretely the TANTGen generator of
\Cref{sec:sol-pipeline} treated as the red team, that manipulates a streaming
flow so as to evade a downstream NIDS. Throughout, the results are reported as measured,
including the sources and perturbation modes on which DEPT performs poorly.

\section{Research Questions}\label{sec:eval-rqs}

The evaluation is organized around two research questions, each tied to one of the
work objectives of \Cref{sec:intro-objectives}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1 (ensemble robustness).} Does heterogeneous stacking, a
    non-differentiable Random Forest fused with a differentiable Temporal Convolutional
    Network, measurably raise the cost of a transferable, gradient-guided evasion attack
    relative to a single-architecture baseline?
    \item \textbf{RQ2 (purification effectiveness).} Does the reconstructor move a
    flagged adversarial flow back onto the benign manifold, closing the detection gap
    seen by the protected NIDS, while emitting a realizable, protocol-compliant packet
    sequence?
\end{itemize}

Both questions are answered by measurement. RQ2 is answered through the
reconstructor's own metrics (\Cref{sec:eval-purification}) and, end to end, through the
deployment baseline that scores the defense against the independent \ac{NIDS} each
attack was built to evade (\Cref{sec:eval-e2e}).

RQ1 needs the second adversary of \Cref{sec:sol-threat}, since TANTGen's gradients come
from its own surrogate and never from DEPT: \Cref{sec:eval-gradablation} differentiates
the \ac{TCN} directly and scores the resulting perturbation, unchanged, against the
Random Forest, the \ac{TCN}, and the fused stack.

A third natural question, whether the full pipeline stays within a
real-time inference budget, is deliberately not addressed here: the wall-clock latency
and overhead evaluation of the delivered system is left to future work
(\Cref{chap:conclusion}).

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:eval-setup}

\subsection{Datasets and Threat Generation}\label{subsec:eval-datasets}

The evaluation corpus is the one of \Cref{sec:sol-pipeline}: ten benign traffic sources
(Mirai and the SSDP flood from Kitsune; DDoS, Port Scan and Brute Force from
CIC-IDS2017; and the five CIC-DDoS-2019 reflection and amplification floods, namely NTP,
DNS, SSDP, SYN, and LDAP), each paired with three TANTGen adversarial variants, one per
perturbation mode (\texttt{both}, \texttt{iat}, \texttt{size}). The per-source
detection tables of this chapter therefore cover ten clean sources and thirty
adversarial source--mode combinations. The label convention of \Cref{sec:sol-pipeline}
applies throughout: raw-capture flows are \emph{benign}, TANTGen-generated ones are
\emph{adversarial}.

The corpus as tensorized comprises $8{,}624{,}958$ flows and $61{,}516{,}406$ packets
across the forty sources (ten benign and thirty adversarial). All models share the
flow-level, source-stratified $70/15/15$ split (seed~$42$) of \Cref{sec:sol-pipeline},
so their results are directly comparable. Because the CIC-DDoS-2019 reflection floods
contribute the overwhelming majority of adversarial flows, the held-out test pool is
adversarial-dominated: of the $1{,}293{,}737$ test flows seen by the TCN and
meta-learner, $1{,}082{,}586$ ($83.7\%$) are adversarial and $211{,}151$ are benign. The
RF, whose first checkpoint fires at packet~$2$, never sees the $6{,}179$ single-packet
flows and therefore scores $1{,}287{,}558$ flows on the streaming axis, which leads to
$1{,}838{,}798$ rows on its per-checkpoint view. This base rate matters when
reading accuracy and precision, and is the main reason the cross-model comparison is
performed by base-rate-robust metrics (AUROC and PR~AUC) and on the
streaming axis rather than on raw accuracy. The reconstructor is counted differently,
since it consumes $(\text{adversarial}\rightarrow\text{clean})$ flow pairs rather than
individual flows: out of the $7{,}217{,}272$ total
adversarial flows, only $2{,}566{,}489$ have a matched five-tuple counterpart, of which
the same split uses $1{,}796{,}542$ pairs for training and holds out $384{,}963$ test
pairs (\Cref{sec:eval-purification}).

\subsection{Implementation Environment}\label{subsec:eval-environment}

All training and evaluation were run on a single server: $2\times$ Intel Xeon
Gold~5320 CPUs @~$2.2$\,GHz ($52$ cores, $104$ threads), $160$\,GB of
RAM, and $2\times$ NVIDIA RTX~6000~Ada GPUs. The
software stack is the one described in \Cref{sec:sol-tools}: PyTorch for the TCN,
meta-learner, and reconstructor; scikit-learn for the Random Forest; and Scapy together
with \texttt{tshark}/\texttt{editcap}/\texttt{tcpdump} for PCAP handling, feature
realization, and protocol-compliance checking.

The full pipeline is expensive to run end to end, and the cost is worth recording.
Generating the thirty
adversarial captures with TANTGen took $26.7$\,h, dominated by the NTP flood alone
($11.9$\,h), and tensorizing the forty sources a further $11.2$\,h. Among the model
stages, the RF completed its eighteen-point grid search and final fit in $2.7$\,h on CPU,
the TCN trained for $24.7$\,h (early-stopping after ten of twenty planned epochs, best
at~$6$), the meta-learner for $3.4$\,h (best epoch $17$ of $20$), and the reconstructor
for $10$\,h (best epoch $19$ of $20$). The two comparison studies cost $4.3$\,h and
$33.6$\,h, the latter because it scores $2{,}704{,}816$ realized flows through the
independent detectors. Repeating the four model stages once per attack
(\Cref{subsec:eval-perattack}) adds roughly $28$\,h.

\section{Metrics}\label{sec:eval-metrics}

The metrics separate into three families.

\paragraph{Static classification.} Judging each unit independently on its
end-of-flow (or per-checkpoint) verdict, the evaluation reports \emph{ROC~AUC} and
\emph{PR~AUC} (threshold-free ranking quality; PR~AUC is the more informative summary
under the $\approx\!83\%$ adversarial base rate), \emph{Macro~F1} (the unweighted mean
of the benign and adversarial $F_1$ at threshold $0.5$), and the per-class
\emph{precision} and \emph{recall}.

\paragraph{Streaming.} A defense that must act before a flow completes cannot be judged
only at end-of-flow. The evaluation therefore reports \emph{AUROC@$t$}: the ROC~AUC
computed using, for each flow, only the prediction available after its first $t$
packets. This exposes how quickly a model becomes discriminative. It is paired with the
\emph{\acf{EDP}}, the median packet index at which a flow's
streaming score first crosses $0.5$, reported with its inter-quartile range, and with
the \emph{detection rate}, the fraction of adversarial flows that cross the
threshold. AUROC@$t$ measures ranking quality; EDP and detection rate measure earliness.

\paragraph{Purification.} The reconstructor is judged on four quantities.
\emph{Reconstruction \ac{MSE}} is computed on the feature dimensions TANTGen actually
perturbed, between the flow and its true clean counterpart, measured before (adversarial
input) and after (reconstructor output). Since the nPrint cells are ternary
($-1$ absent, $0$ unset, $1$ set), a perturbed cell costs $1$ when a present bit flips
and $4$ when a field appears or disappears, so this MSE runs over $[0,4]$ and $1.0$ is
the characteristic bit-flip value, not a ceiling. The headline metric here is
\emph{detection restoration through an independent NIDS}: the clean, adversarial, and
purified (or DEPT-defended) flows are scored through the real NIDS each attack was built
to evade, Kitsune or the ML-NIDS, and the \emph{restoration ratio}, the fraction of the
adversarial-to-clean gap recovered, together with the \ac{ASR} before and after DEPT,
report how much detection the defense returns (\Cref{sec:eval-e2e}). A secondary,
sanity-check metric is the \emph{purification gain}, the drop in DEPT's own TCN's
adversarial-probability $\Delta P(\text{adv})$ from the adversarial to the reconstructed
flow, with its \emph{residual gap} to a genuinely clean flow; this is circular (the
judge is a detector DEPT itself trained) and is not read as an end-to-end claim.
Finally, \emph{protocol
compliance} is the fraction of realized packets that \texttt{tshark} parses without a
malformed-packet error, computed only over packets the realization step can faithfully
emit.

The result on this axis is not the one the design predicted. \emph{The Random Forest
alone is the strongest stream
at every point from packet~$2$ onward}, reaching $0.9805$ at its first checkpoint and
$0.9813$ thereafter, against the meta-learner's $0.9099$ to $0.9334$ and the TCN's
$0.8225$ to $0.8386$. The meta-learner is not the best model in this system; it is
the second-best, and its advantage over the forest is confined to the single first packet.

What the fusion does deliver is narrower than ``the stack beats its base learners'',
and it is threefold. First, at
packet~$1$, where the forest is silent by construction, the meta-learner has the best
available verdict of any model, at $0.8373$ against the TCN's $0.8191$; a defense that
must act on the first packet has only these two options, and fusion is the better one.
Second, the meta-learner dominates the TCN at every step, by $8.7$ to $9.5$ AUROC
points from packet~$2$ onward, so it is clearly better than using the sequence model
alone. Third, and most consequential
for RQ1, the fused stack is measurably harder to evade than either component under a
gradient-guided attacker, which \Cref{sec:eval-gradablation} quantifies: it is the only
one of the three that keeps $74.5\%$ of flows unevaded at every budget. Robustness, not
accuracy on TANTGen's traffic, is the main thing the second base learner adds.

Two further observations complete the picture, and both cut against the fusion. The
meta-learner's per-source behaviour tracks whichever base learner is stronger on that
source, but not reliably: on \texttt{ssdp\_ddos} \texttt{both}/\texttt{iat} it reaches
$0.927$ where the forest reaches $1.000$, and on \texttt{ntp\_ddos}
\texttt{both}/\texttt{iat} it reaches $0.653$--$0.654$ against the forest's $0.999$,
so on those sources it discards most of a signal it was handed. The mechanism is the one
anticipated in \Cref{subsec:sol-meta}: the RF logit enters the fusion token as a single,
sparsely updated feature, refreshed only at checkpoints and absent before packet~$2$,
whereas the dense TCN logit is updated at every timestep, so the meta-learner's attention
systematically under-weights the intermittent evidence. A stacking ensemble should be no
worse than its best base learner; this one is worse on several sources and on the aggregate, and
that is the clearest single target for future work on the detection stage
(\Cref{sec:eval-discussion}).

A note on the auxiliary ``beat the best base'' loss (weight $0.5$,
\Cref{subsec:sol-meta}) closes this section. Over training the auxiliary term falls from
$0.0267$ to $0.0177$, so the meta-learner does move toward matching its best base
learner's confidence; over the same twenty epochs the meta-validation AUROC@$10$ rises
from $0.9197$ to $0.9333$, with essentially all of that gain in the first few epochs.

which is $99.9\%$ of the available gap, while on the eight ML-NIDS sources it moves from
$0.808$ to $0.910$ against a ceiling of $0.987$. However, the Kitsune figure needs a caveat:
$9{,}830$ of its $12{,}417$ flows are \texttt{ssdp\_flood} \texttt{iat}, and Kitsune is scored here on the test flows alone; on the full streams of
\Cref{subsec:eval-e2e-detect}, the only view in which its detection rate is meaningful,
that variant shows no evasion to recover. The
ML-NIDS average is diluted by the many sources where detection was never lost and there
was consequently little to restore.

The sanity-check metric agrees: DEPT's own TCN puts the
reconstruction at $0.428$ against $0.421$ for a genuinely clean flow, a residual gap of
$0.007$.

As with detection, the effect is strongly attack-dependent, so the per-source view is
needed. \Cref{tab:eval-purifsrc} gives the rows that carry the
argument, and \Cref{fig:eval-purifscatter} plots every source--mode pair by its
perturbation footprint (the pre-purification MSE on the perturbed dimensions) against
the gain purification achieves on it. Four outcomes are visible and organized in \Cref{tab:eval-purifsrc}.

Purification is large on the structurally perturbed sources, and these are the
same rows where the independent NIDS records detection regained:
\texttt{ddos\_both} from $0.080$ to $0.865$, \texttt{ddos\_size} from $0.005$ to
$0.817$, \texttt{portscan\_both} from $0.011$ to $0.679$. It is correctly
inert where TANTGen barely moved the features, namely the \texttt{both} and
\texttt{iat} variants of the reflection floods, whose pre-purification MSE is already
near zero. On the \texttt{size} variants of \texttt{ldap\_ddos}, \texttt{ntp\_ddos} and
\texttt{ssdp\_ddos} there is nothing to undo: the attack did not fire at all
there, the adversarial tensor matches the clean one bit for bit, and with no perturbed
cells the footprint is undefined rather than small---the dash in
\Cref{tab:eval-purifsrc}. A gain of $\approx\!0$ on those rows records an attack that
never happened, not a purifier failure.

The third outcome is one where purification demonstrably works but a metric cannot see
it, for two different reasons. On \texttt{dns\_ddos} \texttt{size} the footprint is
maximal and substantially reduced ($1.000 \rightarrow 0.302$), yet the TCN gain stays at
$-0.002$ because that detector assigns adversarial and clean flows the same probability
to begin with: the gain metric is bounded by the detection gap, so on detector-blind
sources purification can seem invisible to it. On \texttt{ssdp\_flood} \texttt{size} the
other column is the one that fails---the footprint is all but erased ($1.000 \rightarrow
0.003$, gain $+0.549$), but the independent NIDS cannot be scored on that stream at all,
because none of its packets survive L7 realization.

The fourth outcome only becomes visible once an independent judge is used. On some
sources where TANTGen never succeeded in evading,
purification actively \emph{harms} detection. Through the independent NIDS,
\texttt{dns\_ddos} falls from $0.986$ to $0.762$ on \texttt{both} and from $0.993$ to
$0.756$ on \texttt{iat}; \texttt{portscan\_iat} falls from $0.994$ to $0.811$;
\texttt{brute\_force\_size} from $0.993$ to $0.963$. The denoiser is doing what it was
trained to do, moving the flow toward the clean manifold, but on these flows the
attack's fingerprint and the clean manifold were never far apart, so what it removes is
signal the protected NIDS was apparently still using.

Two realization caveats must be stated. First, a size edit on an application-layer \emph{text}
protocol (the SSDP/HTTP \texttt{M-SEARCH} messages in \texttt{ssdp\_flood}
\texttt{both}/\texttt{size}) corrupts the L7 grammar even when the IP and UDP lengths
and checksums remain valid, and \texttt{tshark} flags these as malformed. Rather than
count them as compliance failures, the $450{,}758$ such packets are reported
separately as unrealizable at L7, and the $97.22\%$ rate is computed over the
$2{,}412{,}357$ realizable ones. For \texttt{ssdp\_flood} \texttt{size} that exclusion is
total: every packet of that stream is L7-unrealizable, so it has no compliance or
independent-NIDS figure at all. Second, the total compliance rate is pulled down by
just two sources: \texttt{ddos} at $0.84$ ($61{,}447$ of $391{,}270$ realized packets
flagged) and \texttt{syn\_ddos} \texttt{iat} at $0.178$.
Every other source is at or above $0.99$. Neither is a claim about purification quality,
but they do bound how far the traffic-space results can be pushed.

To make these aggregates concrete, one real flow is followed through the pipeline.
\Cref{fig:eval-purifpacket} traces one flow drawn from the held-out test
split, a four-packet Mirai SYN-flood burst from \texttt{192.168.2.108:35220} to
\texttt{52.25.66.250:8280} under the \texttt{size} variant. In the original capture each
datagram is $44$~bytes, a $20$-byte IPv4 header followed by a $24$-byte TCP header with
no application payload. TANTGen appends $29$~bytes of payload to every packet, inflating
the IPv4 total-length field to $73$~bytes; this length shift is the whole perturbation the \texttt{size} variant applies, and it is what pushes the flow off the
clean manifold. The reconstructor recovers the original length, and the traffic-space
realization of \Cref{subsec:sol-realization} trims the appended bytes back off, returning
every datagram to $44$~bytes, a residual of exactly zero on the manipulated field. All
twelve packets across the three stages pass \texttt{tshark} dissection with no malformed
frames and no bad checksums, and because the \texttt{size} variant perturbs length alone
the inter-arrival times are identical between the clean and adversarial captures, so
packet size is the only quantity that changes. The $17.0\times$ reduction in
perturbed-dimension MSE reported above is, at the
granularity of this one packet, this $44 \rightarrow 73 \rightarrow 44$ restoration.

\section{Robustness under a Gradient-guided Attacker (RQ1)}\label{sec:eval-gradablation}

% NOTE: numbers in this section come from
% data/models/gradient_ablation/gradient_ablation_metrics.json
% (scripts/gradient_ablation/run_gradient_ablation.py), full-scale run of 2026-07-20:
% 9,000 flows = 300 per source x 30 sources, 6 budgets, policy fpr_0.05, best-of
% (PGD u random) attacker under the monotone envelope.

Every result so far was produced against TANTGen, which, as stated in
\Cref{sec:sol-threat}, cannot answer RQ1 because it exercises no gradient path into
DEPT: measuring it tells us the ensemble detects \emph{its} perturbations, not what
heterogeneity is worth against an adversary that differentiates one of the ensemble's
members. This section supplies that adversary.

\subsection{The Attacker}\label{subsec:eval-gradablation-attacker}

The attacker is given white-box access to the \ac{TCN} (architecture, weights and
gradients) and nothing for the Random Forest, whose non-differentiability means there is
no gradient to target. It crafts \emph{one} perturbation against the \ac{TCN}, using
\ac{PGD}~\cite{madry2018towards}, and that same perturbation is then replayed, unchanged,
through each model and scored. If the attacker re-optimized per target, it would no
longer be measuring transfer.

The perturbation is \emph{increase-only} padding and delay, per packet, applied on top of
TANTGen's own, so the cost reported is the \emph{extra} budget an attacker must spend. The
padding bytes are drawn at random (\texttt{attack.pad\_byte\_mode}); this is a meaningful
detail, because zero and random padding move the Random Forest's payload-entropy features,
its largest feature group, in opposite directions, while the \ac{TCN}'s gradient sees no
payload bytes and so cannot distinguish the two.
Budgets are denominated in bytes and seconds rather than in an $\ell_\infty$ ball: the \ac{IPAT} channel is standardized with a
standard deviation of $208.2$~seconds while the $1088$ nPrint channels are bits in
$\{-1,0,1\}$, so a single $\epsilon = 0.01$ would buy the attacker ${\pm}2.08$~seconds of
delay and nothing whatsoever in the bit channels. The feasible set (increase-only, integer
bytes, capped at the \ac{MTU}, inert on non-IPv4 packets) is enforced as a projection itself.

Three controls guard the result, because a defense evaluated with a weak attack cannot be fully trusted~\cite{carlini2019evaluating, tramer2020adaptive}. First, a \emph{query-matched
random search}: the same feasible set and budget, an equal number of forward passes, and
no gradient. Second, \emph{AutoPGD}~\cite{croce2020reliable} via the \ac{ART}
library~\cite{nicolae2018adversarial}, a parameter-free optimizer, as an
independent check that the search is not simply under-tuned; it agrees, finding the same
$100\%$ evasion of the \ac{TCN} at the same budget. Third, the reported attacker is the
\emph{best of} \ac{PGD} and random search per flow, so no result rests on a favourite
optimizer.

\subsection{How the Numbers Should Be Read}\label{subsec:eval-gradablation-reading}

Two conventions matter.

\ac{ASR} is measured \emph{only over the flows a target detects unperturbed}. This is the
standard robust-accuracy convention: of the $9{,}000$ sampled flows, the \ac{TCN} detects only $3{,}374$ at
its matched threshold, against $7{,}510$ for the forest and $7{,}597$ for the stack, so an
unconditional \ac{ASR} would mostly be counting flows the detector never caught. A target
that does not detect a flow in the first place cannot be evaded on it.

Thresholds are set at a \emph{matched false-positive rate} on unperturbed benign traffic,
never at a raw $0.5$. The three targets are calibrated very differently, with median
verdicts on benign traffic of $0.043$ for the forest, $0.379$ for the \ac{TCN}, and
$0.002$ for the stack, so a fixed threshold would reward whichever model happens to say
``adversarial'' least often. At a common $5\%$ false-positive rate the thresholds are
$0.4535$, $0.9820$, and $0.3658$ respectively.

Finally, the curves are reported as a monotone envelope over the budget grid. The feasible
sets are nested, so an attacker permitted $1460$~bytes may simply spend $128$, and a
rational attacker's success cannot fall as its budget grows.

\paragraph{The stack costs the attacker more than either base learner alone.} This is RQ1's answer, and
\Cref{tab:eval-gradablation,fig:eval-evasioncost} record it. At the three smallest
non-zero budgets the fused stack concedes $1.7\%$, $4.7\%$, and $8.5\%$ attack success
against the forest's $8.5\%$, $10.7\%$, and $13.1\%$ and the \ac{TCN}'s $40.5\%$,
$50.1\%$, and $52.2\%$; across the whole grid $74.5\%$ of flows never evade the stack at
any budget, against $64.0\%$ for the forest and $45.5\%$ for the \ac{TCN}, whose median
evading flow needs only the smallest budget on the grid. The perturbation that breaks the
\ac{TCN} it was crafted against transfers poorly to the forest, and the fusion of the two
is harder still. For scale, TANTGen's own per-packet padding varies widely by
source and mode, from under a byte to about $290$ bytes: \texttt{mirai\_size} and
\texttt{portscan\_both} average $32$ and $29$ bytes, placing them in the $32$ to $128$
byte region where the stack concedes at most $8.5\%$, whereas the \texttt{size} variants
of \texttt{ddos}, \texttt{ssdp\_flood} and \texttt{portscan} average $289$, $265$ and
$246$ bytes, which puts them nearer the $512$-byte grid point.

The upper end needs a qualification. The ordering is not uniform across the whole
grid: at $512$ bytes the stack ($18.9\%$) and the forest ($18.4\%$) are level, and
the stack pulls ahead again only at $1460$ bytes ($25.5\%$ against $36.0\%$). The claim is
therefore that the stack strictly dominates the \ac{TCN} everywhere and the forest
everywhere except around that one grid point, where they are roughly equal --- and that
grid point is precisely where TANTGen's heaviest-padding sources sit.

\paragraph{The meta-learner shifts its reliance onto the Random Forest as the TCN is
degraded.} This is the design claim of \Cref{subsec:sol-meta} stated as a prediction, and it survives the test. Ablating the meta-learner's two base-learner token
slots, and measuring how far its verdict moves when each is handed back its clean value,
the share attributable to the forest goes from $0.000$ at $\delta = 0$, where there is
nothing to compensate for, to $0.529$ as soon as the attacker spends anything, rising to
$0.563$ at $128$ bytes and staying between $0.54$ and $0.56$ thereafter, with between
$5{,}869$ and $6{,}458$ of the $9{,}000$ flows registering a moved verdict. The stack does
lean on the learner the attacker cannot differentiate. The behaviour is visible even
before any attack: where the \ac{TCN} is blind at $\delta = 0$ and the forest is not, as
on \texttt{ldap\_ddos}, \texttt{ntp\_ddos} \texttt{both}/\texttt{iat},
\texttt{portscan\_iat} and \texttt{syn\_ddos\_iat}, the stack inherits the forest's
detection of $\approx\!1.00$.

\paragraph{Gradient guidance gives this attacker no advantage.} Against the \ac{TCN}, the
query-matched random search does not merely match \ac{PGD}, it beats it at every budget:
$40.1\%$ against $37.2\%$ at $8$ bytes, $52.8\%$ against $50.7\%$ at $1460$. Neither wins
on every flow, though, and since the better of the two is taken per flow, the reported
best-of curve exceeds both ($40.5\%$ and $54.5\%$ at those two budgets): \ac{PGD} does
carry flows the random search misses, which is why the reported attacker is the best of
the two.

\section{End-to-End System Evaluation}\label{sec:eval-e2e}

\subsection{Detect-then-Purify Pipeline}\label{subsec:eval-e2e-detect}

The end-to-end effect of DEPT can be measured through its own detector or through an
independent one. The internal view repeats the purification gain of
\Cref{sec:eval-purification}, with the same circularity; its one addition is that on the
structurally perturbed sources purification is the difference between near-certain
detection ($P(\text{adv})\approx0.99$ on \texttt{portscan} and \texttt{ddos}) and a
near-certain pass ($\approx0.06$--$0.15$). This section therefore goes to the credible
question: what is TANTGen's attack-success rate against the
\emph{independent} NIDS each attack was built to evade, before and after DEPT is placed
in front of it?

\Cref{tab:eval-baseline} reports that deployment baseline. For every attack, three
realized full streams are scored through its true target NIDS: \textbf{Kitsune}
(KitNET~+~AfterImage) for the two Kitsune-native attacks, and TANTGen's own
\textbf{ML-NIDS} (the per-attack CICFlowMeter flow classifiers) for the eight
CIC-IDS2017 and CIC-DDoS-2019 attacks, reusing in each case the very model TANTGen
calibrated its evasion against, so that no DEPT component is ever asked to grade itself.

ceiling (the NIDS working as intended); the \emph{adversarial} stream carries TANTGen's
perturbation and in principle falls to the evasion floor; and the \emph{DEPT-defended} stream is

three streams are realized onto the same adversarial packets, differing only in the size
and timing the purifier touches (\Cref{subsec:sol-realization}). The headline metrics are the attack-success rate (one
minus the NIDS detection rate) before and after DEPT, and the detection-restoration
ratio, the fraction of the adversarial-to-clean detection gap the pipeline recovers.

real and large where TANTGen actually evaded.} On
\texttt{ddos} the pipeline removes $38$ points of attack success and on \texttt{portscan}
$32$, and at mode granularity the strongest cases are \texttt{ddos\_size} ($+67.4$
points), \texttt{portscan\_both} ($+67.0$), \texttt{ddos\_both} ($+59.7$), and
\texttt{portscan\_size} ($+46.8$). These are exactly the structural
\texttt{both}/\texttt{size} edits that the per-source purification results predicted
would be recoverable. \emph{Most combinations are untouched because there was nothing to
recover}: on \texttt{ldap\_ddos}, \texttt{ntp\_ddos}, \texttt{ssdp\_ddos} and
\texttt{brute\_force}, TANTGen never really evaded in the first place (adversarial detection is
already $0.98$ to $1.00$), so the pipeline moves the number by less than a point in
either direction. \emph{And on a fair share of combinations the defense makes matters
worse.} \texttt{dns\_ddos} loses $22.5$ points on \texttt{both} and $22.7$ on
\texttt{iat}, \texttt{portscan\_iat} loses $18.2$, \texttt{ddos\_iat} loses $12.2$, and
\texttt{mirai\_size} loses $23.9$. The mechanism is the one \Cref{sec:eval-purification}
identified: the gate flags essentially all flood traffic, so flows the NIDS was still
catching get purified, and purification removes signals it was using.

The gate's behaviour explains this. Its flag rate is close to $100\%$ on every flood source
and close to zero on several \texttt{size} variants ($0.0\%$ on \texttt{ssdp\_ddos},
$0.2\%$ on \texttt{ntp\_ddos}, $0.8\%$ on \texttt{ldap\_ddos}, $0.9\%$ on
\texttt{dns\_ddos}), which is the same reflection-flood \texttt{size} blind spot the base
learners showed in \Cref{tab:eval-persource}, now visible in the deployed pipeline. One
consequence is that the gated pipeline and an ungated one that purifies everything are
nearly indistinguishable here: the \texttt{purified\_all} upper bound reaches $0.8831$
detection against the gated $0.8836$. The gate as built is therefore doing little work
for these sources, and the finding that matters is that it answers the wrong question:
it predicts \emph{whether a flow is adversarial}, whereas the pipeline needs a predictor
of \emph{whether purifying this flow will help}, which the rows above show is not the
same thing.

Split by detector family, the two halves diverge: the eight ML-NIDS sources gain $9.9$
points of \ac{ASR} reduction over $2{,}622{,}023$ flows, while the two Kitsune sources
lose $1.7$ points over $82{,}793$. The Kitsune figure rests almost entirely on
\texttt{mirai\_size}, where TANTGen's size edit had inadvertently \emph{raised} Kitsune's
detection from $0.003$ to $0.242$, and DEPT undid it, returning detection to $0.003$.
The purifier works correctly there, but the defense loses by it.

Two methodological notes come with these numbers. First, because the defended stream runs
DEPT's detector and purifier over all of an attack's flows for full-volume realism, some
of those flows were seen in training; restricted to the held-out test flows the defended
detection rate is $0.9061$ over $372{,}321$ flows, slightly above the full-stream
$0.8836$, so the deployment view is not flattered by the training overlap. Second,
Kitsune is a partly volumetric detector, so its detection rate is only meaningful on full
streams such as these, which is exactly why the baseline feeds the full realized traffic
rather than a subset.

\subsection{One Global Model or Ten Specialists?}\label{subsec:eval-perattack}

Every result so far concerns a single set of models trained across all ten sources at
once. This raises the question of whether ten specialists, each trained on one attack's
own benign and adversarial flows, as is common in the literature, would do better. The
four model stages were therefore re-run once per attack, giving ten
independent RF/TCN/meta-learner/reconstructor sets, and \Cref{tab:eval-perattack} compares
each specialist meta-learner against the global one on the same slice and the same axis.

The re-run varies the training scope and holds everything else fixed. Each specialist sees
only its own source's benign capture and that source's three adversarial variants, but
inherits the architectures, the grid searches, and the flow-level, seed-$42$ $70/15/15$
split protocol of \Cref{subsec:eval-datasets}, so each is scored on exactly the flows the
global model is scored on once its test set is restricted to that slice. The
variable under test is only what a model may contrast a manipulated flow against, one
source's benign pool or ten.

Neither regime wins outright. On $13$ of the $30$ combinations the two are indistinguishable,
both already at or near $1.000$. Where they differ the pattern is systematic. Specialists
win on the \texttt{both} and \texttt{iat} modes of the reflection floods and on
timing-only edits generally: \texttt{ntp\_ddos} gains $0.346$ and $0.347$,

and \texttt{portscan\_iat} gains $0.066$. Training on one attack lets the model key on
that source's own timing signature instead of averaging over nine unrelated benign pools.
The global model wins on the reflection floods' \texttt{size} variants, but this is a
weak victory, on slices where both regimes are already below chance: it is less
confidently wrong ($0.443$ against $0.192$ on \texttt{dns\_ddos}), not right.

Two findings from this comparison bear on the ensemble design rather than on the
deployment choice. First, the problem of \Cref{sec:eval-meta}, a meta-learner beaten by
its own forest, reproduces itself in the specialists, so it is not an artifact of
training across sources. \texttt{mirai} is the one source where the global model is
genuinely better ($+0.035$, $+0.033$), and it is also where the specialist stack falls
furthest below its own base learner: the per-attack meta-learner reaches $0.705$ overall
while its own Random Forest reaches $0.889$. \texttt{portscan} shows the same sign more
mildly ($0.939$ against $0.999$), and on the remaining eight sources the specialist
meta-learner matches or beats its own forest, so narrowing the training scope neither
worsens this problem nor fixes it. Second, the per-attack TCNs collapse entirely on the
reflection floods, scoring exactly $0.500$ on \texttt{ldap\_ddos} and
\texttt{ssdp\_ddos} and $0.507$ to $0.531$ on the others, so those specialist stacks
rely on their forest alone. On the sources whose flows are long enough to convolve over,
the same specialists are untroubled by the narrower pool ($0.990$ on \texttt{ddos},
$1.000$ on \texttt{ssdp\_flood}, $0.980$ on \texttt{brute\_force}), which suggests the
collapse is due to flow length rather than to training scope, exactly where
\Cref{sec:eval-base} placed the global \ac{TCN}'s version of it.

The practical conclusion is that a single global model is still the right default. A
specialist strategy needs a router that
names the attack before a model can be chosen, which is a version of the very
classification problem the detector exists to solve. Specialists may be worth the trouble in
the cases the table identifies, namely a known high-rate flood whose evasion is expressed
in timing, where \texttt{ntp\_ddos} alone gains $0.346$ and $0.347$.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:eval-discussion}

\paragraph{The research questions.} Both are answered affirmatively: RQ1 outright
(\Cref{sec:eval-gradablation}, bounded by \Cref{subsec:eval-gradablation}), and RQ2 under
an explicit condition, that the attack left a footprint the protected detector could see.
Where it did not, purification ranges from inert to actively harmful, which the rest of
this section takes up. \Cref{sec:concl-rqs} states both answers in full.

\paragraph{Purification without a good gate can subtract as well as add.} This is the
evaluation's most consequential negative finding, and it is visible only under an
independent detector: where TANTGen never actually evaded, purifying \emph{lowers} the
protected NIDS's detection, by up to $22.7$ points on \texttt{dns\_ddos} and $23.9$ on
\texttt{mirai\_size} (\Cref{subsec:eval-e2e-detect}), because there the attack's
fingerprint and the clean manifold were never far apart, so what the denoiser removes is
signal the NIDS was still using. The gate does not prevent this, since it flags nearly
all flood traffic: it predicts whether a flow is adversarial, where the pipeline needs to
predict whether purifying the flow will help.

\paragraph{The reflection floods' \texttt{size} variants fail below chance.} On
\texttt{dns\_ddos}, \texttt{ldap\_ddos}, \texttt{ntp\_ddos} and \texttt{ssdp\_ddos} they
score below chance for both the forest and the meta-learner against same-source benign
flows (\Cref{tab:eval-persource}), so such a flow resembles generic benign traffic
\emph{more} than that flood's own unedited flows do. The cause is not capacity, tuning or
training scope: the per-attack specialists
fail on the same slices (\Cref{tab:eval-perattack}), and the deployed gate flags under
$1\%$ of these flows. On three of the four there is nothing to see at all: on
\texttt{ldap\_ddos}, \texttt{ntp\_ddos} and \texttt{ssdp\_ddos}, every matched test pair
is bit-identical to its clean counterpart (\Cref{sec:eval-purification}), so no detector
can separate those slices, the cost anticipated by the label convention of
\Cref{sec:sol-pipeline}. On the fourth,
\texttt{dns\_ddos}, the purifier cuts a full footprint to under a third without moving
DEPT's own verdict: there, DEPT's $24$ prefix statistics and $1088$ header bits cannot express
whatever separates a \texttt{size}-mode flow of this single-response UDP flood from an
unedited one.

\paragraph{A meta-learner beaten by its own base learner.} The meta-learner's $0.9334$
AUROC sits below the forest's $0.9813$ from packet~$2$ onward
(\Cref{tab:eval-streaming}), and the gap widens on several sources; the likely cause is
the under-weighted forest logit of \Cref{sec:eval-meta}, and the specialist stacks
reproduce it (\Cref{subsec:eval-perattack}). Fusion still provides the only packet-$1$
verdict available and the evasion robustness of \Cref{sec:eval-gradablation}, but a
fusion able to fall back to its best base learner is the clearest improvement available
here.

\subsection{Scope of the Gradient-guided Attack Result (RQ1)}\label{subsec:eval-gradablation}

Three boundaries limit what \Cref{sec:eval-gradablation} accomplishes.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \ac{ASR} is measured over the flows a target detects unperturbed, and at
    $\delta = 0$ the \ac{TCN} detects between nothing and $2\%$ of flows on seven of the
    ten sources, leaving its \ac{ASR} undefined there; four sources have a median of two
    packets against a receptive field of $29$. So that arm of the comparison rests on a
    minority of the corpus.
    \item Some flows are unreachable at any budget. On the \texttt{both} and
    \texttt{size} modes of \texttt{ddos} and \texttt{portscan}, and on
    \texttt{ssdp\_flood\_size}, both the forest and the stack hold the attacker to $0\%$ through
    $1460$~bytes per packet, which says only that padding and delay alone cannot overcome that
    much model confidence.
    \item Since query-matched random search beats \ac{PGD} at every budget, what was
    measured is a search-based attacker that happens to have a gradient available, not
    one that profits from it. The conclusion that a non-differentiable component raises
    evasion cost still holds, since both searches were crafted against the \ac{TCN}.
\end{itemize}
