\fancychapter{Conclusion}

\label{chap:conclusion}

This thesis set out to defend a learning-based \ac{NIDS} against a black-box attacker that
reshapes live attack traffic into protocol-compliant flows the detector labels benign. Its answer
is DEPT, a streaming defense that detects the manipulation mid-flow and undoes it before the
protected system consumes it.

% #############################################################################
\section{Summary of Contributions}\label{sec:concl-summary}

The five contributions of \Cref{sec:intro-contributions} stand as follows. The packet-to-tensor
pipeline (\Cref{sec:sol-pipeline}) turns raw captures into a masked, bit-level view of each flow
with identity fields removed, dense per packet for the \ac{TCN} and sparse statistics for the
\ac{RF}, over one flow-level split that makes the comparisons of \Cref{chap:evaluation}
fair. The heterogeneous ensemble (\Cref{sec:sol-ensemble}) fuses the two at every packet
through an attention-based meta-learner, scored on how early, not only how well, it separates
manipulated from clean flows; the purification stage (\Cref{sec:sol-reconstructor}) pairs a causal
Transformer predicting a residual correction with a realization step that writes it back into
dissector-validated packets, so the protected \ac{NIDS} receives traffic rather than tensors. The
last two are the evaluation itself: the deployment baseline of \Cref{subsec:eval-e2e-detect}, which
judges DEPT through the real \ac{NIDS} each attack was built to evade and compares its purifier with a
published one (\Cref{subsec:eval-sota}), and a reusable ten-source
corpus in three perturbation modes, reported per source and per mode.

\section{Answers to Research Questions}\label{sec:concl-rqs}

\textbf{RQ1 (ensemble robustness)}, from \Cref{sec:eval-rqs}, asked whether heterogeneous stacking
measurably raises the cost of a transferable, gradient-guided evasion attack. It does. Since
TANTGen follows only its own surrogate's gradients, the evaluation used a stronger adversary (\Cref{sec:eval-gradablation}):
white-box access to the \ac{TCN}, nothing for the \ac{RF} and meta-learner, one perturbation against
all three at once. It transfers poorly to the forest, and the stack is costlier still: at the
three smallest budgets it concedes at most $8.5\%$ attack success, against the forest's $13.1\%$
and the \ac{TCN}'s $52.2\%$, and $74.5\%$ of its flows never evade it at any budget against
$45.5\%$ for the \ac{TCN} alone (\Cref{tab:eval-gradablation}). As predicted, the meta-learner
leans on the \ac{RF} as the \ac{TCN} degrades: the forest's share of its verdict rises from zero to
$0.53$ the moment the attacker spends anything.

\textbf{RQ2 (purification effectiveness)} asked whether the reconstructor moves a flagged flow
back onto the benign manifold while emitting a realizable packet sequence. It does, under a
condition: scored through the \emph{independent} \ac{NIDS} each attack was built to evade,
purification recovers $63.0\%$ of the destroyed detection overall, while reconstruction error on the
perturbed dimensions falls by $17.0\times$ and the realized packets pass \texttt{tshark} $97.2\%$
of the time (\Cref{sec:eval-purification}). End to end, the gated pipeline cuts the protected
\ac{NIDS}'s attack-success rate from $21.1\%$ to $11.6\%$ (\Cref{subsec:eval-e2e-detect}). In the same
position, the diffusion-based purifier of Merzouk et al.~\cite{merzouk2024diffusion} recovers $0.5\%$ of
the detection lost where TANTGen evaded, against $63.5\%$ for DEPT (\Cref{subsec:eval-sota}).

The condition is that the manipulation actually evaded. Where it moved the features without evading,
purifying \emph{lowers} the protected detector's detection, by up to $23$ points on
\texttt{dns\_ddos} and $18$ on \texttt{portscan\_iat}, because the denoiser removes signal the
\ac{NIDS} was still using. The aggregate displays large recoveries on five source--mode combinations
against regressions on five others, and the gate does not prevent them: it predicts whether a flow
is adversarial, not whether purifying it will help. RQ2 is answered affirmatively only for flows a
defense would want to purify.

\section{System Limitations}\label{sec:concl-limitations}

Six limitations bound the results of \Cref{chap:evaluation}. The first is
representational, and partly a labelling cost. On the \texttt{size} variants of the DNS, NTP, SSDP and LDAP reflection floods,
every model scores at or \emph{below chance} against same-source benign flows, per-attack specialists
included. On three of the four, the adversarial tokens are bit-identical to the clean ones, so no detector
can tell such a flow from its clean original; on DNS the purifier cuts the footprint without moving DEPT's verdict, so the cause is the
representation itself, its $24$ prefix statistics and $1088$ header bits, not capacity, tuning or training
scope. Since these four floods supply about a third of the adversarial test flows, every aggregate in \Cref{chap:evaluation} averages
near-perfect and below-chance sub-populations.

The second is that purification without an adequate gate subtracts as well as adds, as RQ2's
answer showed; the gate as built barely gates, since purifying every flow yields almost the same
end-to-end result.

The third contradicts the system's own premise. The meta-learner is beaten by its own Random
Forest on the aggregate streaming axis, widely so on several sources (\Cref{sec:eval-meta}), where
a stacking ensemble should be no worse than its best base learner; the carry-forward \ac{RF} logit reaches
the fusion token as one sparsely updated feature that attention under-weights against the dense
\ac{TCN} stream. The fusion
still provides the packet-$1$ verdict and RQ1's robustness, but less than the design hoped for.

The fourth is validation. Purification gain measured through DEPT's own \ac{TCN} is
circular, though the end-to-end claim does not rest on it; each attack is judged by one target
\ac{NIDS} rather than a panel; on \texttt{mirai} a target that detects almost nothing even on the
clean attack; and the defended full-stream figures run DEPT's models over some flows seen in training.
DEPT is also compared with a single published purifier, adapted to its representation, and only
against the attacker it was trained on (\Cref{subsec:eval-sota}).

The fifth is scope. The purifier corrects all $1090$ dimensions without being told which the
attacker moved, but the realization step overlays only the two this threat model perturbs
(\Cref{subsec:sol-realization}), so a flow manipulated in another header field would reach the
protected \ac{NIDS} flagged but unrepaired: ``attack-agnostic'' describes the detection and the
denoiser, not the full deployed pipeline. The feature space is bounded too, to headers, timing,
direction, and payload length and entropy, which stay observable as encrypted traffic grows; payload
content is never parsed, by design, and may be exactly where the blind spot above lives.

The sixth is efficiency. The tensorization is pure Python, slower than the original
C++ nPrint tool it reimplements, setting the throughput floor, and the latency study that would
settle the real-time budget of \Cref{sec:intro-problem} is left undone.

\section{Future Work}\label{sec:concl-future}

The limitations above set most of the agenda.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A gate that predicts benefit rather than adversariality.} The end-to-end
    regressions all follow from purifying flows that were still detected. A gate trained on
    whether purification raises or lowers the protected detector's score, which the system already
    computes, would convert those losses into no-ops, keeping the gains and raising the overall end-to-end performance.
    \item \textbf{An attack-agnostic realization step.} Deriving the fields to edit from the
    residual $\Delta$ itself and reconciling them against the protocol's constraints, in the
    spirit of PANTS's SMT-constrained perturbations~\cite{jin2025robustifying}, would generalize the
    overlay beyond its two hardcoded dimensions.
    \item \textbf{A fusion that never falls below its best base learner.} Re-emitting the \ac{RF}
    evidence at every packet without an explicit staleness weight, or letting the meta-learner fall back
    to the stronger base opinion when the two diverge.
    \item \textbf{More baselines and other attackers.} Repeating the comparison of
    \Cref{subsec:eval-sota} with other purifiers, and against generators DEPT was not trained on,
    would show how much of its advantage comes from training on the same attacker.
    \item \textbf{A latency study.} Timing each stage on live traffic, with tensorization
    moved out of pure Python, would show whether DEPT meets the real-time budget of
    \Cref{sec:intro-problem}.
    \item \textbf{What separates a size-mode DNS flood flow from an unedited one.} Which observable quantity,
    absent from the representation above, distinguishes these flows? Payload content, never parsed by design,
    is the first place to look.
\end{itemize}

The direction this thesis considers most significant, though, is \textbf{continuous adaptation of
the defense}. A static defense, which is what DEPT is once trained, is a fixed target: an
adversary that probes it long enough learns its blind spots and routes around it
(\Cref{chap:related}). Closing the loop means casting the defense as a \ac{RL} agent rewarded by
detection outcomes~\cite{sutton2018reinforcement,nguyen2021deep,caminero2019adversarial}: framed
as a \ac{MDP}, it would sit outside the per-packet critical path, watch the ensemble's verdicts
and the attacker's drift, and retune against an evolving generator such as TANTGen, measured
across attacker generations rather than at one point.

DEPT was built and evaluated on the principle that a defense for learning-based intrusion
detection is only worth measuring against an attack that can exist on the wire. Where it fails, it
fails for reasons the evaluation identifies rather than hides; it is a defense meant to be built
upon, and \ac{RL} is the natural place to pick up from it.
