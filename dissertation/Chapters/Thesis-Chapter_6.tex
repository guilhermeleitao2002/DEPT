\fancychapter{Conclusion}
% The following line allows to ref this chapter
\label{chap:conclusion}

This thesis set out to defend a learning-based \ac{NIDS} against the adversarial threat
in its most realistic form: a black-box attacker that reshapes live attack traffic into
protocol-compliant flows the detector labels benign. The answer it proposed is DEPT, a
streaming defense that detects the manipulation while the flow is still in progress and
undoes it before the protected system consumes the flow. This closing chapter summarizes
what was delivered (\Cref{sec:concl-summary}), answers the research questions that
organized the evaluation (\Cref{sec:concl-rqs}), states the limitations of the delivered
system (\Cref{sec:concl-limitations}), and lays out the directions in which the work most
naturally continues (\Cref{sec:concl-future}).

\section{Summary of Contributions}\label{sec:concl-summary}

Five contributions were announced in \Cref{sec:intro-contributions}; each can now be
restated against the chapters that substantiate it.

The first contribution is the streaming packet-to-tensor pipeline of
\Cref{sec:sol-pipeline}. Raw captures are turned into a masked, bit-level representation
of each flow, in which identity fields are removed so that the models learn behaviour
rather than addresses, and the same representation is emitted at two temporal
granularities, a dense per-packet sequence for the \ac{TCN} and sparse aggregated
checkpoints for the \ac{RF}. Because every model in the system, from the base learners to
the reconstructor, is trained and evaluated on the same flow-atomic split of the same
tensors, the cross-model comparisons of \Cref{chap:evaluation} are like-for-like by
construction, which is a property the pipeline was explicitly designed to provide.

The second contribution is the heterogeneous detection ensemble of
\Cref{sec:sol-ensemble}, an \ac{RF} and a \ac{TCN} from deliberately different
architectural families, fused at every packet by a small attention-based meta-learner,
together with the streaming evaluation axis that scores every model by how early, and not
only how well, it separates manipulated from clean flows. What that axis showed was not
what the design predicted, and the evaluation reports it as measured: the Random Forest
alone ranks flows best from packet~$2$ onward, and the fusion's advantages are narrower
and more specific, namely the only available verdict at packet~$1$, an improvement of
nearly ten AUROC points over using the \ac{TCN} alone, and a measurably higher cost to a
gradient-guided attacker (\Cref{tab:eval-streaming}, \Cref{tab:eval-gradablation}). The
cases where the fusion falls short of its stronger member are recorded with the same
prominence, and taken up in \Cref{sec:concl-limitations}.

The third contribution is the purification stage of \Cref{sec:sol-reconstructor}: a
causal Transformer that predicts a residual correction over a flagged flow, coupled to an
explicit realization step that writes the corrected features back into packets and
validates them with a standard dissector, off the per-packet detection path and scoped
to the two axes this threat model perturbs. Scored through the independent \ac{NIDS} each
attack was built to evade, purification recovers $63.0\%$ of the detection TANTGen had
destroyed, rising to $99.9\%$ on the Kitsune-native sources where the evasion had
genuinely succeeded, at a \texttt{tshark} pass rate of $97.2\%$ over realizable packets
(\Cref{tab:eval-purif}). The defense therefore does not stop at feature space; it hands
back traffic, which was one of the constraints the problem statement imposed from the
start.

The fourth contribution is the deployment baseline of \Cref{subsec:eval-e2e-detect}:
an end-to-end measurement of DEPT as what it is, a defense placed in front of a
protected \ac{NIDS}. The full gated detect-then-purify pipeline is run over clean,
TANTGen-perturbed, and DEPT-defended traffic, and each stream is scored through the
real \ac{NIDS} the attack was built to evade, Kitsune for the Kitsune-native attacks
and TANTGen's ML-NIDS for the CIC attacks, so that the end-to-end claim rests on an
independent judge rather than on DEPT grading itself.

The fifth contribution is the evaluation corpus and its accounting: ten benign traffic
sources drawn from three public datasets, each manipulated by TANTGen in three
perturbation modes, and evaluated per
source and per mode rather than only in aggregate
(\Cref{chap:evaluation}). The corpus itself is reusable for future defense work, and the
per-source analysis is, in the view of this thesis, as much a contribution as the
headline numbers, because it shows that the aggregate hides two very different
populations: sources on which the defense is essentially solved and sources on which no
model rises much above chance.

\section{Answers to Research Questions}\label{sec:concl-rqs}

\Cref{sec:eval-rqs} fixed two research questions, one per pillar of the system. They
can now be answered in turn, with the honesty the evaluation aimed for.

\textbf{RQ1 (ensemble robustness)} asked whether heterogeneous stacking measurably
raises the cost of a transferable, gradient-guided evasion attack. The answer is
affirmative, and it is measured rather than inferred. Since TANTGen is gradient-free and
therefore structurally unable to price a gradient path, the evaluation admitted a second,
stronger adversary: one given white-box access to the \ac{TCN}, given nothing for the
\ac{RF} because a forest has no gradient to give, and required to spend a single
perturbation against all three targets at once (\Cref{sec:eval-gradablation}). Under it,
the perturbation that breaks the \ac{TCN} it was crafted against transfers poorly to the
forest, and the fused stack is costlier still: at the three smallest budgets it concedes
$1.7\%$, $4.7\%$, and $8.5\%$ attack success against the forest's $8.6\%$, $10.7\%$, and
$13.1\%$ and the \ac{TCN}'s $40.5\%$, $50.1\%$, and $52.2\%$, and $74.5\%$ of flows never
evade it at any budget against $45.5\%$ for the \ac{TCN} alone. The design's own
prediction, that the meta-learner would lean on the \ac{RF} as the \ac{TCN} is degraded,
was restated as a falsifiable ablation and survived it: the share of the stack's verdict
attributable to the forest goes from zero at rest to $0.53$ as soon as the attacker
spends anything, and stays there.

Three qualifications keep this from being a broader claim than it is. The ordering is not
uniform across the whole budget grid, since the stack and the forest are level at $512$
bytes before the stack pulls ahead again. The question is only well-posed where the base
learners detect the flow at all, which excludes several sources outright, and on some
sources the models' confidence is simply too large for padding and delay to overcome at
any budget (\Cref{subsec:eval-gradablation}). And, against the grain of RQ1's own
phrasing, the gradient turned out to buy this attacker nothing: query-matched random
search \emph{beats} \ac{PGD} at every budget, because nPrint's binary total-length
encoding makes the loss surface over packet size jagged. What was demonstrated is
therefore that a non-differentiable component raises evasion cost against a strong
search-based attacker, which is a finding about the representation as much as about the
ensemble, and a lead worth pulling (\Cref{sec:concl-future}).

\textbf{RQ2 (purification effectiveness)} asked whether the reconstructor moves a
flagged flow back onto the benign manifold while emitting a realizable packet sequence.
The answer is affirmative, under a condition the evaluation made explicit, and it does
not rest on DEPT grading itself. Scored through the \emph{independent} \ac{NIDS} each
attack was built to evade, purification recovers $63.0\%$ of the destroyed detection
overall and $99.9\%$ on the Kitsune-native sources where TANTGen's evasion had actually
succeeded, while reconstruction error on the perturbed dimensions falls by $17.0\times$
and the realized packets pass \texttt{tshark} $97.2\%$ of the time
(\Cref{sec:eval-purification}). End to end, the gated pipeline cuts the protected
\ac{NIDS}'s attack-success rate from $21.1\%$ to $11.6\%$
(\Cref{subsec:eval-e2e-detect}).

The condition is sharper than earlier drafts of this work assumed, and it is what the
independent judge revealed. Where TANTGen barely moved the features, the purifier is
correctly inert; but where TANTGen moved them without ever evading, purifying the flow
\emph{lowers} the protected detector's detection, by as much as $22$ points on
\texttt{dns\_ddos} and $18$ on \texttt{portscan\_iat}, because what the denoiser removes
is signal the \ac{NIDS} was still using. The aggregate gain is therefore a net of large
recoveries on six source--mode combinations against real regressions on five others, and
the gate that was supposed to prevent the latter does not, since it predicts whether a
flow is adversarial rather than whether purifying it will help. RQ2 is answered
affirmatively for the flows a defense would actually want to purify, and the qualifier
is load-bearing.

\section{System Limitations}\label{sec:concl-limitations}

The evaluation surfaced six limitations, which are collected here without the
surrounding numbers (\Cref{sec:eval-discussion} carries the full account).

The first is a representational blind spot, and it is the sharpest negative result of
this work. On the \texttt{size} variants of the DNS, NTP, SSDP, and LDAP reflection
floods, every model scores \emph{below chance} against same-source benign flows, meaning
a manipulated flow of these floods resembles generic benign traffic more closely than
that flood's own unmanipulated flows do. Three independent observations place the cause
in the feature representation rather than in capacity, tuning, or training scope: models
trained on each attack alone fail on exactly the same slices, the purifier substantially
reduces the perturbation footprint on these sources without moving any detector's verdict,
and the deployed gate flags under $1\%$ of their flows. Whatever distinguishes a padded
single-response \ac{UDP} flood from an unpadded one is not expressible in the $24$ prefix
statistics and $1088$ header bits DEPT observes. Because these floods dominate the corpus
by flow count, the honest reading of every aggregate in
\Cref{chap:evaluation} is an average over near-perfect and below-chance
sub-populations, not a uniform competence.

The second limitation is that purification without an adequate gate can subtract as well
as add. On sources where TANTGen never succeeded in evading, running the purifier
\emph{lowers} the protected \ac{NIDS}'s detection, so the end-to-end gain is a net of
large recoveries on a few source--mode combinations against real regressions on others.
The gate as built barely gates, since purifying every flow yields almost the same
end-to-end result as the gated pipeline, and the underlying problem is that it answers
the wrong question: it predicts whether a flow is adversarial, whereas the pipeline needs
a predictor of whether purification will improve that flow's detectability. This is a
design limitation with a clear remedy, and it is taken up in \Cref{sec:concl-future}.

The third limitation belongs to the fusion design, and it is the one that most directly
contradicts the system's own premise. The meta-learner is beaten by its own Random Forest
on the aggregate streaming axis and by a wide margin on several sources
(\Cref{sec:eval-meta}); a stacker should be no worse than its best base learner, and this
one is worse. The cause was identified, namely that the carry-forward \ac{RF} logit enters
the fusion token as a single sparsely updated feature that the meta-learner's attention
under-weights against the dense \ac{TCN} stream, and the per-attack comparison
(\Cref{subsec:eval-perattack}) shows the pathology worsening when the fusion has less
source diversity to learn from. What the fusion does buy, the packet-$1$ verdict and the
evasion robustness of RQ1, is real but narrower than the design claimed.

The fourth limitation is methodological, and narrower than it was in earlier drafts. The
purification gain and residual gap measured through DEPT's own \ac{TCN} are circular, but
the end-to-end claim no longer rests on them: the deployment baseline
(\Cref{subsec:eval-e2e-detect}) scores the clean, adversarial, and DEPT-defended streams
through the independent NIDS each attack was built to evade, Kitsune or TANTGen's
ML-NIDS. What remains is a residual honesty caveat rather than an open question. The
defended full-stream figures run DEPT's models over some flows seen in training, so they
are paired with a held-out-test breakdown; each attack is judged by a single target NIDS
rather than a panel; on \texttt{mirai} that target detects almost nothing even on the
clean original attack, which makes its rows uninformative in either direction; and
Kitsune's detection rate, being partly volumetric, is read only on the full streams the
baseline feeds. None of these reintroduces the self-reference the TCN-only metric carried.

The fifth limitation is scope, of two kinds. The first is what DEPT can \emph{write
back}: the purifier is attack-agnostic by construction, correcting all $1090$ dimensions
without being told which the attacker moved, but the realization step that returns it to
the wire is not, since it overlays exactly the two quantities this threat model
perturbs, packet size and inter-arrival time (\Cref{subsec:sol-realization}). Against an
adversary that moved some other header field, the feature-space correction would survive
and the traffic-space one would not. Realization sits off the per-packet detection path,
so this bounds what the system can hand back rather than what it can detect, but the
``attack-agnostic'' label belongs to the denoiser alone and is not a property of the
purification stage as a whole.

The second is the feature scope. DEPT observes packet headers, inter-arrival
timing, and direction; it carries no representation of payload content. This choice was
deliberate, since header and timing behaviour remain observable as the share of
\ac{TLS}- and QUIC-encrypted traffic grows while payload inspection does not, but it means
that a manipulation expressed purely in application-layer content, on traffic where
payloads are still readable, would be invisible to the present system. It is also a
candidate explanation for the representational blind spot above, since what separates a padded
single-response flood from an unpadded one may live precisely in the bytes DEPT declines
to look at.

The sixth is an engineering limitation. The tensorization front-end is pure Python,
far slower than the native nPrint tool whose encoding it reimplements, and every
learned stage runs a whole-flow forward pass, since no incremental or KV-cached
inference path was implemented. Neither bound is conceptual, nPrint encoding in
particular is fixed-offset bit slicing that the native tool performs at line rate, but
as delivered they define the system's throughput envelope; quantifying that envelope
is part of the latency and overhead study deferred to \Cref{sec:concl-future}.

\section{Future Work}\label{sec:concl-future}

The limitations above set most of the agenda. Both runs that earlier drafts deferred are
now delivered: the independent-\ac{NIDS} validation of RQ2, as the deployment baseline of
\Cref{subsec:eval-e2e-detect}, and the direct gradient-ablation experiment for RQ1, as
\Cref{sec:eval-gradablation}. What remains on the first is breadth, scoring each attack
against a panel of \ac{NIDS} rather than its single true target; what the second opened up
is more interesting than what it closed.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Is nPrint's encoding hiding the gradient?} The gradient ablation
    found that a gradient-guided attacker barely outperforms random search on the packet-size
    channel, and traced it to nPrint representing size as $16$ binary columns, so that
    adjacent byte counts sit far apart in the model's input space and the loss surface is
    jagged. This is uncomfortably close to \emph{gradient masking}
    (\Cref{subsec:bg-gradient-masking}): an apparent robustness that reflects an attacker's
    difficulty in optimizing rather than a defended decision boundary. Re-running the study
    against a model that consumes packet size as a scalar would separate the two, and the
    answer matters well beyond DEPT, since nPrint-style bit encodings are widely used in
    learned traffic analysis.
    \item \textbf{A stronger attacker for the forest.} The present attacker has no gradient
    into the \ac{RF} by construction, which is the point, but it also means the forest is
    only ever attacked by transfer. A gradient-based attack on a differentiable surrogate
    distilled from the forest, or a decision-tree-specific attack, would test whether the
    forest's resistance is heterogeneity or merely distance from the \ac{TCN}'s gradient.
    \item \textbf{Latency and overhead evaluation.} This thesis confines its
    evaluation to detection and purification quality; the wall-clock side of the
    system, per-flow inference latency, sustained throughput, and the overhead
    purification adds to the detect path, is left to a dedicated follow-on study,
    which will also settle whether the detect-then-purify cycle fits the real-time
    budget the problem statement imposes (\Cref{sec:intro-problem}).
    \item \textbf{A gate that predicts benefit rather than adversariality.} The
    end-to-end regressions of \Cref{sec:concl-limitations} all follow from purifying
    flows that were still being detected. The remedy is a gate trained on a different
    target, namely whether purification raises or lowers the protected detector's score
    for this flow, which is a supervisable quantity since the evaluation harness already
    computes it for every test flow. That change is small, well-specified, and would
    convert several of the reported losses into no-ops.
    \item \textbf{An attack-agnostic realization step.} The purifier learns which
    dimensions to correct without being told; the overlay that returns its output to the
    wire is hand-written for two of them. Closing that gap means deriving the fields to
    edit from the residual $\Delta$ itself, editing whichever header fields it moves
    beyond a threshold and reconciling the result against the protocol's own
    constraints, in the spirit of the SMT-checked perturbations of
    PANTS~\cite{jin2025robustifying}, so that the defense re-emits traffic for
    manipulations it was never designed around. This is the step that would make the
    whole purification path, and not only its model, independent of the attacker.
    \item \textbf{A fusion that never falls below its best base learner.} The
    meta-learner's failure to match its own Random Forest
    (\Cref{sec:concl-limitations}) suggests concrete revisions, from representing the
    \ac{RF} evidence more insistently in the fusion token, for instance by re-emitting
    it at every packet with an explicit staleness weight rather than a single carry-forward
    slot, to gating the stacker so that it can fall back to the stronger base opinion when
    the two diverge.
    \item \textbf{Finding what separates a padded flood from an unpadded one.} The
    below-chance results on the reflection floods' \texttt{size} variants were localized
    to the feature representation rather than to model capacity or training scope, which
    turns a vague failure into a specific question: which observable quantity, absent from
    the $24$ prefix statistics and the $1088$ header bits, distinguishes these flows?
    Answering it would repair the single largest hole in the system's coverage.
    \item \textbf{Payload-aware representation.} Extending the feature space beyond
    headers and timing, where the traffic still permits it, would widen the class of
    manipulations the defense can observe, and is the natural representation-side
    complement to the encryption trend discussed above.
    \item \textbf{A deployment-grade front-end.} Replacing the Python tensorization
    with a native implementation, and giving the models an incremental inference path,
    would let a deployment study test the pipeline against sustained line-rate
    traffic.
\end{itemize}

Beyond these, the direction this thesis considers most significant is
\textbf{continuous adaptation of the defense}. DEPT, once trained, is a static defense,
and a static defense is a fixed target: an adversary that probes it for long enough can
eventually learn its blind spots and route around it, as the arms-race framing of
\Cref{chap:related} makes clear. The natural next step is to close the loop, casting the
defense as a \ac{RL} agent for which detection outcomes are the reward
signal~\cite{sutton2018reinforcement,nguyen2021deep,caminero2019adversarial}. Framed as
a \ac{MDP}, such an agent would sit outside the per-packet critical path, observe the
ensemble's verdicts and the drift in the attacker's behaviour, and retune the defense
over time against a generator, such as TANTGen, that is itself evolving. Its success
would be measured not at a single point but across attacker generations, as a slower
degradation of detection quality and a lower end-to-end attack success rate than a
frozen copy of the present system. Every component DEPT already has, the streaming
verdicts, the purifier, and the paired corpus, is a building block of that loop; what
this thesis delivers is the static defense the adaptive one would start from.

In closing, this work argued that a defense for learning-based intrusion detection is
only worth measuring against an attack that can exist on the wire, and it built and
evaluated one under that discipline end to end: a shared traffic representation,
architecturally heterogeneous detection, and purification that returns real,
protocol-compliant packets. Within its stated limits, the delivered system detects a
realistic black-box manipulation from the first packet and undoes it before the
protected system consumes the flow, and where it fails, it fails for reasons the
evaluation identifies rather than hides. That, for a defense meant to be built upon, seemed the right place to leave it.
