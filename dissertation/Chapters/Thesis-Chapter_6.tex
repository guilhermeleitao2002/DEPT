\label{chap:conclusion}

This thesis set out to defend a learning-based \ac{NIDS} against a black-box attacker that
reshapes live attack traffic into protocol-compliant flows the detector labels benign. Its
answer is DEPT, a streaming defense that detects the manipulation mid-flow and undoes it
before the protected system consumes it.

\section{Summary of Contributions}\label{sec:concl-summary}

Five contributions were announced in \Cref{sec:intro-contributions}; each can now be
restated against the chapters that substantiated them.

The first is the packet-to-tensor pipeline of \Cref{sec:sol-pipeline}, which turns
raw captures into a masked, bit-level representation of each flow, identity fields removed so
that the models learn behaviour rather than addresses, at two granularities: a dense
per-packet sequence for the \ac{TCN} and sparse aggregated checkpoints for the \ac{RF}. Every
model is trained and evaluated on the same flow-atomic split of the same tensors, so the
comparisons of \Cref{chap:evaluation} are like-for-like by construction.

The second is the heterogeneous detection ensemble of \Cref{sec:sol-ensemble}, an \ac{RF} and
a \ac{TCN} from deliberately different architectural families fused at every packet by a small
attention-based meta-learner, together with the streaming axis that scores every model by how
early, and not only how well, it separates manipulated from clean flows. That axis did not
show what the design had predicted, and the evaluation reports it as measured: the Random Forest
alone ranks flows best from packet~$2$ onward, and the fusion's advantages are narrower, the
packet~$1$ verdict and a higher cost to a gradient-guided attacker
(\Cref{tab:eval-streaming}, \Cref{tab:eval-gradablation}).

The third is the purification stage of \Cref{sec:sol-reconstructor}: a causal Transformer that
predicts a residual correction over a flagged flow, linked afterwards to a realization step that writes
the corrected features back into packets and validates them with a dissector
(\Cref{tab:eval-purif}). Realization closes the inference path, so the protected \ac{NIDS}
receives traffic rather than tensors, at the cost of being scoped to the two axes this threat
model perturbs. The defense therefore does not stop at feature space, as the problem statement
required from the start.

The fourth is the deployment baseline of \Cref{subsec:eval-e2e-detect}, which measures DEPT as
what it is, a defense in front of a protected \ac{NIDS}: the gated pipeline is run over clean,
TANTGen-perturbed, and DEPT-defended traffic, each stream scored through the real \ac{NIDS}
the attack was built to evade, so that the end-to-end claim rests on an independent judge
rather than on DEPT grading it itself.

The fifth is the evaluation corpus and its honest accounting: ten traffic sources from three public
datasets, each manipulated by TANTGen in three perturbation modes and evaluated per source and
per mode rather than only in aggregate (\Cref{chap:evaluation}). The corpus is reusable, and
the per-source analysis is as much a contribution as the headline numbers, because the
aggregate hides two very different populations: sources on which the defense is essentially
solved and sources on which no model rises much above chance.

\section{Answers to Research Questions}\label{sec:concl-rqs}

\Cref{sec:eval-rqs} fixed two research questions, one per pillar of the system. Both can now
be answered.

\textbf{RQ1 (ensemble robustness)} asked whether heterogeneous stacking measurably raises the
cost of a transferable, gradient-guided evasion attack. It measurably does. Because TANTGen is gradient-free and cannot measure a gradient path, the evaluation
employed a stronger adversary: white-box access to the \ac{TCN}, nothing for the \ac{RF} since
a forest has no gradient to give, and a single perturbation spent against all three targets at
once (\Cref{sec:eval-gradablation}). That perturbation transfers poorly from the \ac{TCN} to
the forest, and the fused stack is costlier still: at the three smallest budgets it concedes $1.7\%$, $4.7\%$, and $8.5\%$ attack success against the forest's
$8.5\%$, $10.7\%$, and $13.1\%$ and the \ac{TCN}'s $40.5\%$, $50.1\%$, and $52.2\%$, and
$74.5\%$ of flows never evade it at any budget against $45.5\%$ for the \ac{TCN} alone. The
design's prediction that the meta-learner would lean on the \ac{RF} as the \ac{TCN} degrades survived: the forest's share of the stack's
verdict goes from zero at the beginning to $0.53$ as soon as the attacker spends anything, and stays
there.

Three qualifications bound the claim. The ordering is not uniform: stack and forest are level
at $512$ bytes before the stack pulls ahead again; the question is only well-posed where the
base learners detect the flow at all, which excludes several sources, and elsewhere confidence
is too large for padding and delay to overcome at any budget
(\Cref{subsec:eval-gradablation}); and, against RQ1's own phrasing,
the gradient bought this attacker nothing, since query-matched random search \emph{beats}
\ac{PGD} at every budget because nPrint's binary total-length encoding makes the loss surface
over packet size jagged. What was demonstrated is therefore that a non-differentiable
component raises evasion cost against a strong search-based attacker, a finding that owes as
much to the representation as to the ensemble architecture (\Cref{sec:concl-future}).

\textbf{RQ2 (purification effectiveness)} asked whether the reconstructor moves a flagged flow
back onto the benign manifold while emitting a realizable packet sequence. It does, under a
condition the evaluation made explicit, and the answer does not rest on DEPT grading alone.
Scored through the \emph{independent} \ac{NIDS} each attack was built to evade, purification
recovers $63.0\%$ of the destroyed detection overall and $99.9\%$ on the Kitsune-native
sources where TANTGen's evasion had actually succeeded, while reconstruction error on the
perturbed dimensions falls by $17.0\times$ and the realized packets pass \texttt{tshark}
$97.2\%$ of the time (\Cref{sec:eval-purification}). End to end, the gated pipeline cuts the
protected \ac{NIDS}'s attack-success rate from $21.1\%$ to $11.6\%$
(\Cref{subsec:eval-e2e-detect}).

The condition is, however, specific. Where TANTGen barely moved the features the purifier is correctly unmoved; where it moved them
without evading, purifying actually \emph{lowers} the protected detector's detection, by as much
as $22$ points on \texttt{dns\_ddos} and $18$ on \texttt{portscan\_iat}, because what the
denoiser removes is signal the \ac{NIDS} was still using. The aggregate gain is therefore a
net of large recoveries on six source--mode combinations against real regressions on five
others, and the gate meant to prevent the latter does not, since it predicts whether a flow is
adversarial rather than whether purifying it will help. RQ2 is answered affirmatively only for the
flows a defense would actually want to purify.

\section{System Limitations}\label{sec:concl-limitations}

The evaluation surfaced six limitations, collected here without their numbers
(\Cref{sec:eval-discussion} carries the full analysis).

The first is a representational blind spot. On the
\texttt{size} variants of the DNS, NTP, SSDP, and LDAP reflection floods, every model scores
\emph{below chance} against same-source benign flows: a manipulated flow resembles generic
benign traffic more closely than that flood's own unmanipulated flows do. Three independent
observations place the cause in the representation rather than in capacity, tuning, or
training scope: models trained on each attack alone fail on exactly the same slices, the
purifier reduces the perturbation footprint there without moving any verdict, and the deployed
gate flags under $1\%$ of their flows. Whatever distinguishes a padded single-response \ac{UDP}
flood from an unpadded one is not expressible in the $24$ prefix statistics and $1088$ header
bits DEPT observes. Since these floods dominate the corpus by flow count, every aggregate in
\Cref{chap:evaluation} is an average over near-perfect and below-chance sub-populations, not
uniform competence.

The second is that purification without an adequate gate can subtract as well as add, as RQ2's
discussion showed. The gate as built barely gates, since purifying every flow yields almost the
same end-to-end result, and it answers the wrong question: whether a flow is adversarial, not
whether purification will improve its detectability.

The third belongs to the fusion design, and most directly contradicts the system's own
premise: the meta-learner is beaten by its own Random Forest on the aggregate streaming axis,
and by a wide margin on several sources (\Cref{sec:eval-meta}), where a stacker should be no
worse than its best base learner. The carry-forward \ac{RF} logit enters the fusion token as a
single sparsely updated feature that attention under-weights against the dense \ac{TCN}
stream, and the per-attack comparison (\Cref{subsec:eval-perattack}) shows the pathology
worsening with less source diversity. What the fusion does buy, the packet-$1$ verdict and the
robustness of RQ1, is real but narrower than the design hoped to achieve.

The fourth is methodological, but narrower than the previous ones. Purification gain and
residual gap measured through DEPT's own \ac{TCN} are circular, but the end-to-end claim
does not rest on them: the deployment baseline (\Cref{subsec:eval-e2e-detect}) scores clean,
adversarial, and defended streams through the independent NIDS each attack targets. What
remains is a specific weaker check: the defended full-stream figures run DEPT's models over
some flows seen in training; each attack is
judged by a single target NIDS rather than a panel of them; on \texttt{mirai} that target detects
almost nothing even on the clean attack, leaving its rows uninformative; and Kitsune's partly
volumetric detection rate is read only on full streams.

The fifth is scope, of two kinds. The purifier is attack-agnostic, correcting all $1090$
dimensions without being told which the attacker moved, but the realization step overlays
exactly the two quantities this threat model perturbs, packet size and inter-arrival time
(\Cref{subsec:sol-realization}). Against an adversary that
moved another header field, the feature-space correction would survive and the traffic-space
one would not, and that flow would reach the protected \ac{NIDS} unrepaired, flagged with
nothing to hand back; the ``attack-agnostic'' label therefore belongs to detection and to the
denoiser, not to the deployed pipeline as a whole. The feature space is bounded too: DEPT observes
headers, timing, and direction, with no direct representation of payload. That was deliberate, since
header and timing behaviour remain observable as encrypted traffic grows while payload
inspection does not, but a manipulation expressed purely in application-layer content would be
invisible, and payload may be exactly where the blind spot above lives.

The sixth is engineering. The tensorization front-end is pure Python, far slower than the
native nPrint tool whose encoding it reimplements, and every neural stage runs a whole-flow
forward pass, since no incremental or KV-cached inference path was implemented. Neither bound
is conceptual, nPrint encoding being fixed-offset bit slicing the native tool performs at line
rate, but as delivered they define the system's throughput floor
(\Cref{sec:concl-future}).

\section{Future Work}\label{sec:concl-future}

The limitations above set most of the agenda. What remains on the independent-\ac{NIDS}
validation of RQ2 (\Cref{subsec:eval-e2e-detect}) is breadth, a panel of \ac{NIDS} rather
than each attack's single true target; the gradient ablation for RQ1
(\Cref{sec:eval-gradablation}) opened up more than it closed.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Is nPrint's encoding hiding the gradient?} The ablation found a
    gradient-guided attacker barely outperforming random search on the packet-size channel,
    and traced it to nPrint encoding size as $16$ binary columns, so that adjacent byte counts
    sit far apart and the loss surface is jagged. This is uncomfortably close to \emph{gradient
    masking} (\Cref{subsec:bg-gradient-masking}); re-running the study against a model that
    consumes size as a scalar would separate the two, and the answer matters wherever
    nPrint-style bit encodings are used.
    \item \textbf{A stronger attacker for the forest.} The forest, having no gradient by
    construction, is only ever attacked by transfer. A gradient-based attack on a differentiable
    surrogate distilled from it would test whether its resistance is actually heterogeneity or merely
    distance from the \ac{TCN}'s gradient.
    \item \textbf{Latency and overhead evaluation.} Per-flow and per-packet inference latency, sustained
    throughput, and the overhead purification adds to the detect path are left to a following
    study, which would also settle whether the detect-then-purify cycle fits the real-time
    budget of \Cref{sec:intro-problem}.
    \item \textbf{A gate that predicts benefit rather than pure adversariality.} The end-to-end
    regressions above all follow from purifying flows that were still being detected. A gate
    trained instead on whether purification raises or lowers the protected detector's score, a
    measure the system already computes for every test flow, would convert several reported
    losses into no-ops.
    \item \textbf{An attack-agnostic realization step.} The purifier learns which dimensions
    to correct without being told; the overlay that returns its output to the wire is
    hand-written for two of them. Closing that gap means deriving the fields to edit from the
    residual $\Delta$ itself and reconciling them against the protocol's constraints, in the
    spirit of PANTS's SMT-checked perturbations~\cite{jin2025robustifying}, so that the defense
    re-emits traffic for manipulations it was never designed around.
    \item \textbf{A fusion that never falls below its best base learner.} The meta-learner's
    failure to match its own Random Forest suggests re-emitting the \ac{RF} evidence at every
    packet with an explicit staleness weight rather than in a single carry-forward slot, or
    gating the stacker so that it falls back to the stronger base opinion when the two diverge.
    \item \textbf{Finding what separates a padded flood from an unpadded one.} Narrowing down the
    below-chance results to the feature representation turns a failure into a specific
    question: which observable quantity, absent from the $24$ prefix statistics and the $1088$
    header bits, distinguishes these flows? Answering it would repair the largest hole in the
    system's coverage.
    \item \textbf{Payload-aware representation.} Extending the feature space beyond headers and
    timing, where the traffic still permits it, would widen the class of manipulations the
    defense can observe.
    \item \textbf{A deployment-grade front-end.} Replacing the Python tensorization with a
    native implementation, like DPDK, and giving the models an incremental inference path, would let a
    deployment study test the pipeline against sustained line-rate traffic.
\end{itemize}

Beyond these, the direction this thesis considers most significant is \textbf{continuous
adaptation of the defense}. A static defense, which is what DEPT is once trained, is a fixed
target: an adversary that probes it long enough learns its blind spots and routes around it,
as the arms-race framing of \Cref{chap:related} makes clear. The natural next step is to close
the loop, casting the defense as a \ac{RL} agent for which detection outcomes are the reward
signal~\cite{sutton2018reinforcement,nguyen2021deep,caminero2019adversarial}. Framed as a
\ac{MDP}, it would sit outside the per-packet critical path, observe the ensemble's verdicts
and the attacker's drift, and retune the defense against an evolving generator such as
TANTGen, its success measured across attacker generations rather than at a single point. Every component DEPT already has is a building block of that loop; what this thesis
delivers is the static defense the adaptive one would start from.

In closing, this work argued that a defense for learning-based intrusion detection is only
worth measuring against an attack that can exist on the wire, and it built and evaluated one
under that principle end to end. Within its stated limits, DEPT detects a realistic black-box
manipulation from the first packet and undoes it before the protected system consumes the
flow; where it fails, it fails for reasons the evaluation identifies rather than hides. It is a
defense meant to be built upon, and \ac{RL} is the right place to leave it.
