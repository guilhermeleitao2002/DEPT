DEPT combines three main ingredients: a bit-level representation of network traffic, a
heterogeneous detection ensemble, and a generative purifier. This chapter presents the
concepts that each of these components builds upon. Instead of a broad survey of machine
learning for security, the focus is kept on the specific concepts that the system relies
on, so that the design decisions of \Cref{chap:solution} follow from the principles
introduced here. \Cref{sec:bg-ml-nids} covers learning-based intrusion detection, traffic
representation, and the model architectures that DEPT composes. \Cref{sec:bg-aml}
introduces adversarial machine learning and the realizability constraints that distinguish
it in the network domain. \Cref{sec:bg-ensembles} presents ensemble learning and the
transferability argument behind DEPT's detector, and \Cref{sec:bg-summary} ties the three
parts together.

\section{Machine Learning for NIDS}\label{sec:bg-ml-nids}
A \ac{NIDS} inspects network traffic in order to separate benign activity from attacks.
Classical systems match traffic against hand-written signatures of known threats, which
makes them precise on catalogued attacks but unable to detect anything absent from the rule
set, including minor variants of existing malware. \ac{ML} reformulates detection as a
data-driven problem: instead of enumerating signatures, a model learns the statistical
structure that separates benign from malicious traffic, and can therefore generalize to
variants and to previously unseen attacks~\cite{boutaba2018comprehensive,wang2021machine}.
This shift supports a large body of modern detection
research~\cite{xu2025deep,anis2025survey}, and it defines the setting in which DEPT
operates: every component of the system is a learned model.

\subsection{Learning Paradigms}\label{subsec:bg-paradigms}
Learning-based intrusion detection is usually organized by the kind of supervision a model
requires. Four paradigms recur in the literature, of which DEPT draws on two.

\emph{Supervised learning} trains on labelled data, where each example is marked benign or
malicious, and learns a mapping from input features to labels (\Cref{fig:bg-paradigms}).
Random forests, support vector machines, and deep neural networks all follow this
paradigm~\cite{boutaba2018comprehensive,sarker2021deep}, and it is the main approach in
DEPT: the two base detectors, the meta-learner that fuses them, and the purifier are all
trained under supervision. \emph{Unsupervised learning} uses no attack labels. It models
the normal behaviour of a network and flags departures from that baseline as anomalies,
which makes it attractive for catching zero-day attacks that a supervised model never saw
during training. The canonical example in this domain is Kitsune, an ensemble of
autoencoders that scores each packet by how poorly it reconstructs under a model of benign
traffic~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune}. DEPT does not train a dedicated anomaly detector, but the
same principle, learning the distribution of clean traffic and measuring deviation from it,
reappears in its purifier. \emph{Semi-supervised learning} sits between the two, exploiting
a small labelled set together with abundant unlabelled data; it is especially relevant in
networking, where labelling traffic at scale is expensive. Finally, \emph{reinforcement
learning} replaces the fixed dataset with an agent that learns by interacting with an
environment and receiving rewards. DEPT employs neither of the last two, although
reinforcement learning is the natural framework for the continuous-adaptation extension
discussed in \Cref{chap:conclusion}.

\subsection{Feature Engineering and Representation}\label{subsec:bg-features}
The performance of a learned detector depends directly on the representation it consumes.
Feature engineering, the process of turning raw packets into numerical vectors, places an
upper bound on what any model can learn~\cite{salim2026feature,ning2024study}. Before
discussing representations, one term should be fixed: throughout this document, a
\emph{flow} is the sequence of packets that share the same five-tuple (source and
destination \ac{IP} addresses, source and destination ports, and transport protocol), ordered by
arrival time.

Two axes organize the design space of traffic representations. The first is
\emph{granularity}. \emph{Static}, packet-level features are read from individual headers
(addresses, ports, flags); they are cheap enough to extract at line rate, but they carry no
temporal context. \emph{Dynamic}, flow-level features aggregate a sequence of packets into
statistics such as mean packet size, inter-arrival times, or flow duration. These capture
behaviour, but at the cost of latency, since the system must wait for the flow to
progress~\cite{apruzzese2023sok}. The second axis concerns the space in which a
perturbation is expressed: the \emph{traffic-space} of actual packets on the wire versus
the \emph{feature-space} of derived vectors. This distinction becomes central once
adversaries are considered (\Cref{subsec:bg-realizability}). DEPT works at both
granularities, and deliberately keeps its features close to the traffic-space so that its
outputs can be turned back into real packets.

Rather than hand-designing features, DEPT represents traffic with \textbf{nPrint}, a
bit-level encoding of packet headers~\cite{holland2021nprint}. Hand-crafted feature sets
usually assume in advance which fields matter, and they are tied to particular protocols: a
tool that computes flow statistics for \ac{TCP} says nothing useful about an \ac{ICMP} flood. nPrint
instead maps each bit of a packet's headers to one feature in a fixed-width vector, using a
tri-state encoding: a value of $1$ or $0$ records a header bit that is set or unset, and
$-1$ marks a field that belongs to a protocol layer absent from that packet. The
representation is uniform across protocols, requires no manual feature design, and can be
consumed directly by models that learn their own features. Bit-level encodings of this kind
have proven effective for traffic analysis in settings where hand-crafted features
generalize poorly~\cite{holland2021nprint,wang2017end,lotfollahi2020deep}. DEPT encodes the
IPv4, TCP, \ac{UDP}, and ICMP headers, producing a $1088$-bit vector ($480 + 480 + 64 + 64$),
and appends two derived per-packet scalars, the z-scored \ac{IPAT} and a direction bit, to
obtain a $1090$-dimensional per-packet token. \Cref{fig:bg-nprint} illustrates the
encoding. This choice places DEPT within a broader movement towards learned traffic
representations, such as the byte-level Deep Packet~\cite{lotfollahi2020deep},
one-dimensional convolutional encoders over raw flows~\cite{wang2017end}, and pre-trained
datagram transformers~\cite{lin2022bert}.

A bit-level header vector inevitably includes fields that identify the particular hosts and
ports of a capture: the source and destination IP addresses, the transport ports, and the
IP identification field. If these fields are left intact, a model can reach high benchmark
accuracy simply by memorizing which addresses and ports appear in the attack traces,
instead of learning the attack behaviour itself; this shortcut inflates test scores but
does not survive deployment on a different network~\cite{arp2022dos,apruzzese2023sok}. DEPT
therefore \emph{masks} these identity fields, zeroing the $144$ bits that encode them
(\Cref{fig:bg-nprint}). Masking forces the detectors to rely on behavioural structure, such
as packet sizing and timing, and it directly supports the claim that detection transfers
across deployments instead of overfitting to one capture. Field selection of exactly this
kind is a first-class feature of the nPrint representation~\cite{holland2021nprint}.

The same tokens feed two representations at different rates, a design choice matched to the
two kinds of base learner that DEPT uses (\Cref{tab:bg-streams}). The \ac{TCN} consumes the
full per-packet token stream, one $1090$-dimensional vector per packet. The random forest
instead consumes \emph{prefix-checkpoint statistics}: at selected packet positions, six
base features (inter-arrival time, packet size, payload length, payload entropy, TCP flags,
and direction) are summarized by their mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum over
the flow prefix seen so far, giving a compact $24$-dimensional vector. Checkpoints are
emitted at packet~$2$ and then every tenth packet. The early checkpoint is deliberate: some
attacks generate extremely short flows. A port scan, for example, averages roughly two
packets per flow, typically a single SYN and its RST, so without the checkpoint at
packet~$2$ the forest would never issue a verdict for precisely the flows that a scan
consists of. Feeding one learner a dense per-packet sequence and the other a sparse,
aggregated summary is also a source of the ensemble diversity that \Cref{sec:bg-ensembles}
argues to be valuable.

\subsection{Model Architectures Relevant to DEPT}\label{subsec:bg-architectures}
DEPT combines three architecture families, chosen for complementary properties rather than
for raw accuracy alone.

\paragraph{Random forests.}
A \ac{RF} is an ensemble of decision trees trained by bagging (\Cref{subsec:bg-ensemble-types}):
each tree recursively partitions the feature space with axis-aligned threshold tests, and
the forest averages their votes~\cite{breiman2001random,breiman1996bagging}. Forests are a
strong, low-cost fit for tabular and statistical features and need no feature
scaling~\cite{lucas2023comprehensive}, which is why DEPT applies one to the
$24$-dimensional checkpoint vectors. Their decisive property for this work, however, is
structural: a forest's decision function is constant over axis-aligned regions and
therefore \emph{non-differentiable}. It exposes no informative gradient that an attacker
can follow, a property whose relevance for adversarial robustness is developed in
\Cref{subsec:bg-gradient-masking}.

\paragraph{One-dimensional and temporal convolutional networks.}
A \ac{CNN} slides learnable kernels over its input to detect local patterns; in one
dimension it processes sequences, extracting recurring motifs from a packet stream such as
header patterns or sizing signatures~\cite{wang2017end,lotfollahi2020deep}
(\Cref{fig:bg-1dcnn}). A \ac{TCN} specializes this design for long sequences through two
devices~\cite{bai2018empirical}. \emph{Dilated} convolutions insert a growing stride
between kernel taps, so the receptive field expands with depth without a corresponding
blow-up in parameters~\cite{oord2016wavenet}. \emph{Causal} convolutions pad the input so
that the output at position $t$ depends only on positions $\leq t$, never on future
packets. Causality is what makes the model valid for streaming inference: it can emit a
verdict after each packet arrives. DEPT's TCN stacks three residual blocks of $128$
channels with kernel size $k = 3$ at dilations $1$, $2$, and $4$, with two convolutions per
block. Its receptive field is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{RF} \;=\; 1 + 2\,(k-1)\sum_{i=0}^{2} 2^{\,i}
              \;=\; 1 + 2\cdot 2\cdot(1+2+4) \;=\; 29 \text{ packets},
    \label{eq:bg-rf}
\end{equation}
so a verdict at any packet integrates evidence from up to the preceding $29$
(\Cref{fig:bg-tcn}).

\paragraph{Transformers.}
The self-attention mechanism lets every position in a sequence attend to every other
position, weighting them by a learned measure of
relevance~\cite{vaswani2017attention}. Each input position is projected into a query, a
key, and a value vector; collecting these as the rows of matrices $Q$, $K$, and $V$,
attention computes
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Attention}(Q, K, V) \;=\; \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V,
    \label{eq:bg-attention}
\end{equation}
where $d_k$ is the key dimension, so the output at each position is a weighted combination
of the values of the positions it attends to. This captures long-range dependencies that a
fixed convolution window cannot. Two arrangements are relevant here. \emph{Encoder-only}
models attend bidirectionally over the entire sequence, which suits classification and
representation tasks, including learned traffic
analysers~\cite{lin2022bert,ghadermazi2025gtae}. \emph{Decoder-only} models apply a causal
mask so that each position attends only to the past, which suits autoregressive and
streaming generation (\Cref{fig:bg-transformer}). Causality also enables \emph{key--value
caching}: since the keys and values of past positions in \Cref{eq:bg-attention} do not
change when new tokens arrive, they can be stored and reused, and producing each new token
then costs work proportional to the current length instead of reprocessing the whole
prefix. This property is what keeps causal, autoregressive inference tractable under tight
latency budgets. DEPT adopts the causal arrangement in both of its Transformer components:
the meta-learner is a compact, causally-masked encoder that fuses the base detectors'
per-packet outputs (\Cref{subsec:bg-ensemble-types}), and the purifier is a decoder-style
sequence model whose causal masking keeps it amenable to key--value caching for streaming
deployment (\Cref{chap:solution}).

\section{Adversarial Machine Learning}\label{sec:bg-aml}
The same flexibility that lets a learned model generalize also exposes it to manipulation.
An \emph{adversarial example} is an input perturbed by a small, deliberate amount so that
the model misclassifies it, often while remaining nearly indistinguishable from a clean
input~\cite{szegedy2014intriguing,goodfellow2014explaining}. For a \ac{NIDS}, adversarial
examples are the mechanism of \emph{evasion}: an attacker reshapes malicious traffic just
enough for it to be labelled benign~\cite{he2023adversarial,ennaji2025adversarial}.
\ac{AML} studies both how such manipulations are constructed and how models can be hardened
against them.

\subsection{Threat Taxonomy}\label{subsec:bg-threat-taxonomy}
Attacks are classified along two axes: what the adversary knows, and what the adversary
wants.

Along the knowledge axis, a \emph{white-box} attacker has full access to the model,
including its architecture and weights, and can compute gradients of the loss with respect
to the input to craft precise perturbations. A \emph{gray-box} attacker knows only part of
the pipeline, such as the feature set. A \emph{black-box} attacker can only query the
system and observe its outputs. The black-box setting is the realistic one for a deployed
\ac{NIDS}, which an attacker probes as an oracle, and such attacks typically rely on
\emph{transferability}: an adversarial example crafted against a substitute model often
fools the target as well~\cite{papernot2017practical}. Along the goal axis, \emph{poisoning}
corrupts the model during training, whereas \emph{evasion} manipulates inputs at inference
time against an already-trained model. DEPT is designed for the black-box evasion threat,
which is the case that dominates real deployments (\Cref{chap:solution}).

Network intrusion detection is harder to attack, and to defend, than the image
classification setting where adversarial examples were first studied, because of a
constraint with no analogue in the pixel domain. Any pixel of an image can be modified
slightly and the result is still a valid image. A network feature vector cannot be modified
freely: a perturbation must correspond to a packet or flow that is actually valid on the
wire. Recovering a realizable packet from a desired feature vector is the \textbf{\ac{IFM}}
problem, and it is frequently constrained or computationally
intractable~\cite{pierazzi2020intriguing,hashemi2019towards,sun2023gpmt}. The \ac{IFM}
problem is the defining difficulty of network adversarial machine learning, and it shapes
attacks and defenses alike. In particular, the gradient-based methods that dominate the
image setting, such as the \ac{FGSM} and
\ac{PGD}~\cite{goodfellow2014explaining,madry2018towards}, produce feature perturbations
that need not correspond to any sendable packet, which limits their direct applicability in
this domain.

\subsection{Defense Paradigms}\label{subsec:bg-defense-paradigms}
Defenses against evasion fall into a few broad families, with different strengths for
traffic data. \emph{Adversarial training} augments the training set with adversarial
examples so that the model learns to classify them correctly~\cite{madry2018towards}. It is
effective against the perturbations it is trained on, but it is costly, it can depress
accuracy on clean traffic, and in networking it is only meaningful if the adversarial
examples are themselves realizable rather than impossible feature vectors. DEPT follows
this line in spirit, since its detectors are trained on realizable adversarial traffic
rather than on synthetic feature perturbations (\Cref{chap:solution}); the fundamental
difference is that DEPT's goal is to distinguish naturally occurring traffic from
adversarially perturbed traffic, not to classify both as malicious.

\emph{Input transformation}, or \emph{purification}, is the family DEPT builds its purifier
on. Instead of hardening the classifier, it transforms each input before classification in
order to remove the perturbation, projecting the sample back onto the manifold of normal
traffic, as \Cref{fig:bg-purification} illustrates. The idea originates with Defense-GAN,
which projects each input onto the manifold captured by a generative adversarial
network~\cite{samangouei2018defense}, and it has since been realized in the network domain
with diffusion models~\cite{alhussien2024advpurrec,li2025adbm} and reconstruction-based
Transformer autoencoders~\cite{ghadermazi2025gtae}. DEPT's purifier is a causal Transformer denoiser in
this tradition (\Cref{chap:solution}).

Two further families should be mentioned, although DEPT adopts neither. \emph{Defensive
distillation} trains a second model on the softened probability outputs of a first one,
smoothing the decision surface so that useful gradients become harder to
find~\cite{papernot2016distillation}; it has been largely circumvented and does not address
realizability. \emph{Certified robustness} provides provable guarantees that no
perturbation within a bounded region can change a prediction, through randomized
smoothing~\cite{cohen2019certified,yan2025certta} or Lipschitz and edit-distance
bounds~\cite{abad2025lipslev}. Such guarantees are valuable, but they are difficult to
scale to the high-dimensional, discrete, and sequential structure of network traffic, and
they secure a formal radius rather than the traffic-space validity that is central to this
work.

\subsection{Realizability and Protocol Constraints}\label{subsec:bg-realizability}
The gap between a \emph{valid feature vector} and a \emph{valid packet} is the central
difficulty of network adversarial machine learning. A perturbation can move a feature
vector anywhere in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, but a packet must satisfy hard structural constraints:
checksums must match the bytes they cover, header-length fields must equal the actual
lengths, control flags must be mutually consistent, and sequence numbers must be ordered. A
perturbation that ignores these constraints is \emph{unrealizable}: it describes no packet
that can be sent, so evaluating a detector against it overstates the real
threat~\cite{pierazzi2020intriguing,hashemi2019towards}. Recent attack research enforces
realizability explicitly, whether by verifying every perturbation against protocol
constraints with an SMT solver, as in PANTS~\cite{jin2025robustifying}, or by generating
traffic that is protocol-compliant by construction, as in GPMT~\cite{sun2023gpmt} and
TANTGen~\cite{pissarra2026tantgen}.

It is important to note that the constraint applies to both sides, and this is what makes
it a design concern for DEPT rather than a property of attacks alone. A defense that
\emph{edits} traffic, as a purifier does, must itself re-emit valid packets; otherwise its
output is unusable, or is trivially detected as malformed. The step from feature-space back
to traffic-space, which we call \emph{realization}, is therefore treated as a first-class
part of the design rather than a mere afterthought. The nPrint representation is an asset
here: because it is close to a bijective, bit-level image of the packet headers, a purified
nPrint vector can be decoded back into a concrete packet and checked for on-the-wire
validity, which DEPT does with packet-level tooling (\Cref{chap:solution}). The same
protocol constraints can even be turned into a detector, by flagging inter-field
inconsistencies~\cite{kumar2025nids}; DEPT does not adopt this approach, but it underlines
how central protocol structure is to the problem.

\section{Ensemble Learning for Robustness}\label{sec:bg-ensembles}
An \emph{ensemble} combines several base models into a single predictor, on the premise
that a collective decision is more accurate and more robust than that of any single
learner~\cite{lucas2023comprehensive,bibers2025ensemble}. Beyond accuracy, ensembles are of
particular interest for security, because diversity among the base models can be turned
directly against adversarial transferability.

\subsection{Bagging, Boosting, Stacking}\label{subsec:bg-ensemble-types}
Three combination strategies dominate (\Cref{fig:bg-ensembles}). \emph{Bagging} trains
homogeneous models in parallel on bootstrap resamples of the data and averages their
predictions, which reduces variance; the random forest is bagging applied to decision
trees~\cite{breiman1996bagging,breiman2001random}. \emph{Boosting} trains models
sequentially, each one focusing on the examples its predecessors got wrong, which reduces
bias~\cite{freund1997decision}. \emph{Stacking} trains \emph{heterogeneous} base learners
and then trains a separate \emph{meta-learner} to combine their
outputs~\cite{wolpert1992stacked}. Stacking is most effective precisely when the base
learners are diverse and make different kinds of error, so that their combination covers
more of the input space than any of them alone.

DEPT stacks a random forest and a temporal convolutional network, which is precisely the
setting stacking was designed for. The two learners are heterogeneous in architecture and
asynchronous in operation: the network emits a dense verdict on every packet, while the
forest emits a sparse verdict only at its checkpoints, starting at packet~$2$. A fixed
combination rule, such as averaging or majority voting, cannot express when each learner
should be trusted. A learned meta-learner can: by assigning input- and position-dependent
weights to each base output, it acts as a dynamic gating mechanism rather than a static
average~\cite{yang2025novel,gungor2022stewart}. DEPT implements this meta-learner as a
small attention model over the fused per-packet outputs, a choice that is further motivated
by the transferability argument below and detailed in \Cref{chap:solution}.

\subsection{Gradient Masking and Transferability}\label{subsec:bg-gradient-masking}
Transferability is what makes black-box evasion practical: an attacker who cannot see the
target model builds a differentiable substitute, crafts a perturbation against it with a
gradient method such as \ac{PGD} or the \ac{FGSM}, and relies on that perturbation carrying
over to the target~\cite{papernot2017practical}. Heterogeneous stacking acts directly on
this mechanism. A differentiable member, such as the \ac{TCN}, exposes a gradient that an
attacker can follow. An \ac{RF} does not: its decision function is constant over
axis-aligned regions, so there is no gradient direction to exploit. Combining a
differentiable and a non-differentiable model yields what the literature calls a rugged
decision boundary, in which a perturbation optimized against the differentiable member does
not necessarily move the forest's discrete verdict, and therefore transfers poorly across
the ensemble, raising the attacker's
cost~\cite{yang2021trs,gungor2022stewart,biggio2011bagging,chen2022collective}.

This argument must be stated carefully, because the absence of a usable gradient is a form
of \emph{gradient masking}, and gradient masking is not robustness in the general case. A
determined white-box adversary can estimate gradients numerically or fall back on
gradient-free search, and masked gradients have repeatedly produced a false sense of
security when mistaken for genuine robustness~\cite{athalye2018obfuscated}. The argument
holds for DEPT precisely because its threat model is black-box: the value of a
non-differentiable member is not an absolute barrier, but a measurable reduction in the
transferability from whatever substitute the attacker is able to build without direct
gradient access. Robustness is therefore treated as a property to be evaluated empirically,
not as a guarantee to be assumed, a distinction that \Cref{chap:evaluation} takes up.

\section{Summary}\label{sec:bg-summary}
The concepts in this chapter compose into the three pillars of DEPT.
\emph{Representation}: a bit-level nPrint encoding, stripped of identity fields and emitted
at two rates, gives heterogeneous learners a shared, behaviour-focused, and realizable view
of traffic (\Cref{subsec:bg-features}). \emph{Ensembling}: heterogeneous stacking, fused by
an attention meta-learner, combines a differentiable network with a non-differentiable
forest to resist transferable gradient attacks under a black-box threat model
(\Cref{sec:bg-ensembles}). \emph{Purification}: an input-transformation defense, built as a
causal Transformer, projects flagged traffic back onto the benign manifold while respecting
the protocol constraints that keep its output realizable
(\Cref{subsec:bg-defense-paradigms,subsec:bg-realizability}). These three elements are the
building blocks of the architecture presented in \Cref{chap:solution}. The prior work that
each of them draws on, and the gap that DEPT's particular combination fills, are the
subject of \Cref{chap:related}.
