\label{chap:related}

The tension between learning-based detection and adversarial evasion has produced a
large body of research on both sides. \Cref{chap:back} introduced the concepts this
work builds on; this chapter surveys the concrete systems, and reads each of them
against the questions DEPT has to answer. The survey is selective rather than
exhaustive: a work is emphasized when it shapes a design decision of DEPT, or when some
part of DEPT departs from it.

\section{Adversarial Attacks against ML-based NIDS}\label{sec:rel-attacks}
A defense is only meaningful relative to the attacks it expects, so the survey starts
on the offensive side. Following the distinction of \Cref{subsec:bg-features}, attacks are
divided by the space in which they operate: the feature-space of derived vectors, or
the traffic-space of actual packets.

\paragraph{Feature-space attacks.}
The first wave of attacks against learned traffic detectors used the
gradient-based methods of the image domain, \ac{FGSM} and \ac{PGD} among
them~\cite{goodfellow2014explaining,madry2018towards}, onto \ac{NIDS} feature vectors.
Hashemi et al.~\cite{hashemi2019towards} were among the first to evaluate
state-of-the-art network detectors under such manipulations, and Tiki-Taka~\cite{zhang2020tiki}
made the threat concrete at scale: attacking deep learning-based intrusion detectors
with a handful of black-box methods, it reached evasion rates of up to $35.7\%$
against models that looked nearly perfect on clean benchmarks. Two lessons carry
over to this work. First, a detector built from a single differentiable architecture
inherits a smooth loss surface that gradient-guided search can exploit, directly or
through a substitute model~\cite{papernot2017practical}; notably, the defenses that
Tiki-Taka itself proposed, model voting and ensemble adversarial training, already
point towards the ensemble route DEPT takes. Second, most of these attacks perturb
feature vectors without asking whether any real packet sequence maps to the perturbed
vector, which is exactly the realizability constraint of
\Cref{subsec:bg-realizability}~\cite{pierazzi2020intriguing,apruzzese2023sok}: their
reported evasion rates are therefore upper bounds obtained in a space an external attacker does
not control.

\paragraph{Traffic-space attacks.}
The recent attack literature has therefore moved into the traffic-space, enforcing
realizability either by verification or by construction.
\textbf{GPMT}~\cite{sun2023gpmt} generates practical malicious traffic with a \ac{GAN}
under little prior knowledge of the target, constraining its perturbations so that the
traffic keeps its malicious function. \textbf{PANTS}~\cite{jin2025robustifying}
couples adversarial search with a \ac{SMT} solver, so that every candidate
perturbation is formally checked against protocol and semantic constraints before it
is accepted.
\textbf{DeepRed}~\cite{hajizadeh2025deepred} embeds a \ac{GAN}-based generator in a command-and-control framework and shows that realizable, protocol-abiding
attacks degrade the detection rate of ML-based \ac{NIDS} by up to $20\%$, which
settles that the threat survives the realizability constraint. Most recently,
\textbf{NetDiffuser}~\cite{kumar2026netdiffuser} brought diffusion models to the
attack side, injecting semantically consistent perturbations into flow features,
although still under a white-box assumption. The trend across these works is clear:
the attack side has converged on generative sequence models operating under explicit
realizability constraints, and it is precisely to this class that the attacker of this
thesis belongs.

\subsection{TANTGen, the Attacker}\label{subsec:rel-tantgen}
\textbf{TANTGen}~\cite{pissarra2026tantgen} is a Transformer-based adversarial network
traffic generator developed in a sibling thesis, and it gets a closer treatment than the
works above because it is the concrete adversary that DEPT is trained against and
evaluated on. Where GPMT and
DeepRed rely on \ac{GAN}s and PANTS on solver-checked perturbations, TANTGen uses
self-attention to model the sequential dependencies between the packets of a flow, and
generates its evasions in the traffic-space directly, so that protocol compliance
holds by construction rather than only by after-the-fact verification. It operates in the
black-box setting: it needs no access to the target detector's architecture, weights,
or gradients, only to a surrogate \ac{NIDS} of its own, trained on labelled traffic, whose
gradients guide the generator. Its evasion was demonstrated against the detectors its
generation was calibrated on: Kitsune~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune}, an anomaly-based
detector, for the Kitsune-native attacks, and its own ML-NIDS flow classifiers for the
CIC attacks. The manipulation itself is
confined to two quantities of live attack traffic, packet timing and packet
size, exposed as three perturbation modes (\texttt{iat}, \texttt{size},
\texttt{both}) that structure the corpus of \Cref{sec:sol-pipeline}.

Taking TANTGen as the adversary has three consequences that run through DEPT's design.
First, since the adversarial traffic is protocol-compliant, a defense
gains nothing from checking protocol validity; whatever signal exists must be found in
the traffic's behaviour, its timing and sizing structure, which is what DEPT's
learned detectors consume. Second, because the perturbation touches only timing and
size, the detectable footprint can be thin, especially in the timing-only mode;
\Cref{chap:evaluation} quantifies this. Third, the black-box setting
makes transferability the operative attack channel, which is the scenario the
heterogeneity argument of \Cref{subsec:bg-gradient-masking} is aimed at. The threat
model of \Cref{sec:sol-threat} formalizes these assumptions.

\subsection{Gradient-guided Evasion, the Second Attacker}\label{subsec:rel-gradient}
TANTGen is the adversary DEPT is trained on, but it cannot be the only one it is
evaluated against. DEPT's central design claim is that fusing a non-differentiable
Random Forest with a differentiable \ac{TCN} raises the cost of evasion because an
attacker's gradient reaches only one of the two. TANTGen's gradients come from its own
surrogate, never from DEPT, so it can show that the ensemble detects its perturbations
but not measure this claim. \Cref{sec:eval-gradablation} closes that gap with a
complementary adversary drawn from the standard adversarial-\ac{ML} toolkit.

The canonical instrument is \emph{\ac{PGD}}~\cite{madry2018towards}, which takes
repeated gradient steps against a model's loss and projects each iterate back onto a
feasible set. Its strength as an evaluation tool is that the projection step states the
attacker's budget explicitly. This matters because a weak attack and a
strong defense are indistinguishable in the reported numbers, and many published
defenses have been shown to owe their apparent robustness to badly-configured attacks
rather than to any real property~\cite{carlini2019evaluating, tramer2020adaptive}.
\Cref{sec:eval-gradablation} therefore reports its attack-strength controls alongside
its results, including a cross-check against the parameter-free
\emph{AutoPGD}~\cite{croce2020reliable} of the \ac{ART} library~\cite{nicolae2018adversarial}.

Two features of the network-traffic setting keep this from being a routine application
of those tools. The perturbation is not a free vector in the model's input space: it must
remain a sendable packet, so the attacker's variables are bytes of padding and seconds of
delay, and the budget must be denominated in those units rather than in an $\ell_p$ ball. And the ensemble's two halves
do not even read the same features, so a perturbation crafted against one must be
replayed through the other's featurisation to be scored at all. \Cref{sec:eval-gradablation}
respects both constraints, and \Cref{subsec:eval-gradablation} discusses what they
imply for how the result should be read.

\section{Training-time Defenses}\label{sec:rel-training}
Training-time defenses harden a model before it is ever deployed, by sanitizing its
training data or by shaping its decision surface. Four lines of work are relevant
here.

\paragraph{Data sanitization and poisoning defenses.}
The earliest defensive line protects the integrity of the training set itself.
\textbf{Antidote}~\cite{rubinstein2009antidote} replaces the principal-component
subspace of an anomaly detector with robust statistics, so that poisoned flows that
deviate from the benign subspace are rejected instead of taken in; its acknowledged
difficulty is telling adversarial poisoning apart from the benign drift that network traffic produces naturally. A complementary result concerns
bagging: Biggio et al.~\cite{biggio2011bagging} showed empirically, and Chen et
al.~\cite{chen2022collective} later certified, that training each ensemble member on a
random subset of the data dilutes the influence of any individual poisoned sample
across the collective decision. DEPT excludes poisoning from its threat model
(\Cref{sec:sol-threat}), so these defenses are not competitors; they matter here
because they establish, from the training side, the same principle DEPT exploits at
inference time, that ensemble diversity is a robustness mechanism in itself and not only an
accuracy one.

\paragraph{Federated and backdoor defenses.}
As \ac{NIDS} deployments move towards \ac{FL} for privacy, a parallel line defends the
training pipeline itself, detecting backdoors in the model updates clients contribute,
in one case by having clients validate candidates inside \acp{TEE}~\cite{wan2025energy,xu2025detecting,rieger2024crowdguard},
and unlearning them from weights already
compromised~\cite{zhu2023selective,kumari2023baybfed,nguyen2025pbp}.
These too defend a different adversary than DEPT's, and are complementary rather than
competing.

\paragraph{Ensemble-based hardening.}
A third line builds robustness into the model by combining several learners, which is
the training-time ancestor of DEPT's detection stage. Beyond Tiki-Taka's voting
ensembles~\cite{zhang2020tiki}, \textbf{TRS}~\cite{yang2021trs} showed that low
transferability among ensemble members can be promoted directly, by regularizing for
gradient diversity and model smoothness during training, and
\textbf{Stewart}~\cite{gungor2022stewart} demonstrated, in the domain of
predictive maintenance, that a stacking ensemble with a learned meta-model withstands
white-box attacks substantially better than its individual members.
\textbf{Mateen}~\cite{alotaibi2024mateen} takes the ensemble idea in the direction of
distribution shift, maintaining an adaptive committee of autoencoders for network
anomaly detection, and broader benchmarking frameworks confirm the accuracy benefits
of ensembling for intrusion detection in general~\cite{bibers2025ensemble}. What
nearly all of these have in common is that their members are drawn from the same
differentiable family, so a gradient exists for every member and the ensemble's
protection rests on decorrelating those gradients. Heterogeneity across families,
mixing a non-differentiable forest with a differentiable network so that part of the
ensemble offers no gradient at all, is far less explored, and it is the specific form
of diversity DEPT's detector is built on (\Cref{sec:sol-ensemble}).

\paragraph{Realizable adversarial training.}
Adversarial training~\cite{madry2018towards} hardens a model by adding adversarial
examples into its training set, but in the network domain it is only as good as the
examples themselves: a model trained against impossible feature perturbations is
hardened against a threat that cannot occur, the \ac{IFM} problem of
\Cref{subsec:bg-threat-taxonomy} seen from the defender's side. The works of
\Cref{sec:rel-attacks} close this gap by supplying realizable adversarial data, and
PANTS in particular demonstrates that inserting its solver-verified examples into the
training loop yields robustness that persists against attacks not seen during
training~\cite{jin2025robustifying}, while DeepRed argues the same necessity from the
red-team side~\cite{hajizadeh2025deepred}. DEPT's corpus construction follows this
line: its detectors are trained on realizable, black-box adversarial traffic generated
by TANTGen, not on synthetic feature noise (\Cref{sec:sol-pipeline}). The goal,
however, differs. Adversarially trained classifiers aim to keep classifying
perturbed attacks as attacks; DEPT aims to recognize the \emph{manipulation} itself,
and then to undo it, which places its second stage clearly in the inference-time family
surveyed next.

\section{Inference-time Defenses}\label{sec:rel-inference}
Inference-time defenses act at deployment, on the inputs a model receives. Three
groups are relevant: certifying predictions against bounded perturbations, purifying
inputs before classification, and detecting adversarial inputs by auxiliary signals.

\paragraph{Certified robustness.}
Certification promises that no perturbation within a bounded region can flip a
prediction. \textbf{CertTA}~\cite{yan2025certta} made randomized smoothing practical
for traffic analysis by drawing its smoothing noise jointly over the dimensions an
attacker actually perturbs, packet timing, sizing, and insertion, and derives
robustness regions that are meaningful for traffic models.
\textbf{LipsLev}~\cite{abad2025lipslev} obtains deterministic certificates under a
bounded Levenshtein distance in a single forward pass, several orders of magnitude
faster than smoothing-based verification. The guarantee is valuable but bounded by
construction: a certificate covers a radius, and a TANTGen-style manipulation is not
constrained to stay inside one, since a flow's timing can be stretched by legitimately
large amounts while remaining perfectly realizable.

\paragraph{Generative purification.}
The purification family goes back to \textbf{Defense-GAN}~\cite{samangouei2018defense},
which projects each input onto the manifold captured by a generative model before
classification. Its modern descendants are mostly diffusion-based and mostly proven in
the image and text domains~\cite{li2025adbm,zhang2024causaldiff,li2025diffusedef}, where
``causal'' denotes factor attribution rather than the temporal masking DEPT uses. In the
network domain the idea has been instantiated more
recently: \textbf{AdvPurRec}~\cite{alhussien2024advpurrec} reconstructs traffic
features with a diffusion model before they reach the \ac{NIDS}, Merzouk et
al.~\cite{merzouk2024diffusion} map the trade-off that governs such purifiers, where
more diffusion noise removes more of the perturbation but degrades clean accuracy, and
\textbf{GTAE-IDS}~\cite{ghadermazi2025gtae} applies a graph-Transformer
autoencoder to reconstruct traffic representations for real-time detection.

DEPT's reconstructor (\Cref{sec:sol-reconstructor}) shares this family's goal but
departs from it on three axes. The first is
architectural: a diffusion purifier needs the whole input before it can denoise it, and
its cost grows with the number of denoising steps, whereas DEPT uses a causal Transformer
that corrects each packet from the packets before it, in a single pass, and
defaults to the identity (no-op) through its residual head. The trade is explicit: DEPT gives
up the broad distribution coverage of a diffusion process in exchange for a model that
fits streaming deployment. The second axis is supervision: purifiers in the
Defense-GAN tradition learn from clean data alone and purify by projection, while DEPT
exploits the matched adversarial-to-clean flow pairs its corpus provides, a stronger
form of supervision that is normally unavailable, but that the paired-generator setting
of TANTGen provides; \Cref{subsec:eval-sota} compares the two directly, with the purifier
of Merzouk et al. as the baseline. The third axis is the one this thesis considers decisive: every network
purifier surveyed above stops at the feature vector, and none of them re-emits
traffic. A purified vector that corresponds to no sendable packet sequence has simply
moved the realizability problem from the attacker to the defender. DEPT treats the
step back into the traffic-space, realization followed by dissector-level validation,
as part of the defense itself (\Cref{subsec:sol-realization}).

\paragraph{Constraint-based and auxiliary detection.}
A final group detects adversarial inputs without a generative process.
\textbf{NIDS-CBAD}~\cite{kumar2025nids} flags violations of inter-feature domain
constraints, for instance a payload size inconsistent with a packet count, on the
observation that naive feature-space attacks often break such invariants.
\textbf{AMDS}~\cite{olukola2026amds} fuses ensemble disagreement, predictive
uncertainty, and distributional anomaly scores into an adversarial-input detector with
attack-adaptive weights. DEPT adopts neither as its detection principle, for a reason
that follows directly from \Cref{subsec:rel-tantgen}: its adversary emits
protocol-compliant traffic by construction, so constraint checks are blind to it, and
the constraint family defends against exactly the naive attacks that the realizability
literature has already moved past. The disagreement signal of AMDS is closer in
spirit to this work: DEPT's meta-learner can in fact be read as a learned,
per-packet version of it, fusing the opinions of two deliberately different base
learners (\Cref{subsec:sol-meta}).

\section{Positioning of DEPT and Gap Analysis}\label{sec:rel-positioning}
\Cref{fig:rel-landscape} arranges the surveyed defenses along the two axes that
organized this chapter, the phase at which a defense acts and the space in which it
operates, and \Cref{tab:rel-comparison} records the same works with their strategies
and techniques. Read together, they make the pattern of the literature visible. The
training-time column is well populated, but almost entirely in the feature-space;
PANTS is the exception that carries realizability into training. The inference-time
work concentrates on purification, yet every network purifier acts on feature vectors
and returns feature vectors. Traffic-space at inference time exists, in
CertTA's certified regions, but certification bounds a radius rather than undoing a
manipulation.

The gap this thesis addresses is the combination. Of the surveyed works, none brings
together (a) training data that is realizable and generated under a black-box threat
model, (b) an ensemble that is heterogeneous across architectural families, pairing a
non-differentiable with a differentiable learner to resist transfer, and (c) a
purification stage whose output is realized back into protocol-compliant
packets. Each property exists in isolation: PANTS holds (a) but hardens classifiers
and performs no purification, TRS and Stewart hold (b) but within homogeneous
differentiable families or outside the network domain, and AdvPurRec and GTAE-IDS hold
(c) up to the feature vector, on single datasets and without a streaming constraint.
DEPT composes all three and is evaluated end to end on a corpus built over ten
traffic sources and three perturbation modes, with metrics that respect the streaming setting
(\Cref{chap:evaluation}). One further line is acknowledged but not pursued:
reinforcement learning is increasingly used to make security responses
adaptive~\cite{ren2025arcs,wilson2025pro,qiao2025slot}, and retraining against an
evolving generator is the natural continuation of this work. DEPT as delivered is a
static defense.
