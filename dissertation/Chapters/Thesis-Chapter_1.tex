\label{chap:intro}

Network intrusion detection has largely become a machine learning problem. The systems
that guard today's networks rely less and less on hand-written signatures and more on
learned models, and with that shift they inherited a weakness that signatures never had:
the models themselves can be attacked. This thesis is concerned with that weakness in its
most realistic form, and with what a defender can actually do about it. It proposes DEPT
(Defense via Ensemble and Purification Transformer), a streaming defense that detects
adversarially manipulated network traffic and undoes the manipulation before it reaches
the detector it was meant to evade.

\section{Context and Motivation}\label{sec:intro-context}
A \ac{NIDS} watches the traffic that crosses a network boundary and decides what is
malicious. For a long time this decision was made by matching traffic against signatures
of known threats, which is precise for catalogued attacks and blind to everything else.
\ac{ML} changed the terms of the problem: instead of enumerating what an attack looks
like, a model learns the statistical behaviour that separates malicious from benign
traffic, and can therefore generalize to variants and to attacks it never
saw~\cite{boutaba2018comprehensive,wang2021machine}. Learning-based detection is by now
the dominant research direction in the field and an increasingly common deployment
choice~\cite{xu2025deep,anis2025survey}.

The improvement came with a new attack surface. A learned model can be fooled: a small,
deliberate perturbation of its input may flip its decision, the phenomenon known as an
\emph{adversarial example}~\cite{szegedy2014intriguing,goodfellow2014explaining}. For a
\ac{NIDS}, adversarial examples take the form of \emph{evasion}: the attacker reshapes
ongoing attack traffic just enough for the detector to label it benign, while the attack
keeps doing its work~\cite{he2023adversarial}. The threat is not hypothetical. Black-box
attacks have reached evasion rates of up to $35.7\%$ against deep detectors that looked
nearly perfect on clean benchmarks~\cite{zhang2020tiki}. \Cref{fig:intro-evasion}
illustrates the situation this thesis starts from.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includesvg[width=0.98\textwidth]{./Images/intro_evasion}
    \caption[The adversarial evasion problem.]{The problem this thesis addresses. In the
    form the attacker originally generates it, attack traffic is flagged by the ML-based
    NIDS that guards the network (top path). A black-box generator, TANTGen in this work,
    reshapes the same attack, altering only packet timing and sizes while every packet
    remains protocol-compliant, and the detector now labels the traffic benign, so the
    attack is delivered to the network it was aimed at (bottom path). DEPT exists to
    close exactly this gap.}
    \label{fig:intro-evasion}
\end{figure}

Not every adversarial example in the literature is a real threat, however, and this
point shapes the whole thesis. In the image domain, where adversarial examples were
first studied, any perturbed pixel array is still an image; a perturbed feature vector
of network traffic, in contrast, is only a threat if some sendable sequence of packets
actually produces it. Recovering concrete traffic from a desired feature vector is the
\ac{IFM} problem, and it is often intractable, since packets must keep their checksums,
lengths, and protocol semantics consistent~\cite{pierazzi2020intriguing,hashemi2019towards}.
A realistic attacker is then confined to the \emph{black-box} setting: it can probe a
deployed detector and observe outcomes, but it holds neither the model's architecture
nor its gradients~\cite{papernot2017practical,apruzzese2023sok}. Much of the defensive
literature sets aside one or both of these constraints, assuming white-box access or
training against feature-space perturbations that no real packet sequence can produce,
and so ends up measuring robustness against a threat that cannot occur as measured;
\Cref{chap:related} examines this mismatch in detail.

This mismatch is what the present work, together with its sibling thesis, set out to
remove. TANTGen~\cite{pissarra2026tantgen}
closes the realism gap on the attack side: it is a Transformer-based generator that
manipulates attack traffic under black-box constraints, altering only packet timing
and packet size, and the traffic it emits is protocol-compliant by construction. DEPT is
the defense side of the same effort. It is trained on, and evaluated against, the
realizable adversarial traffic that TANTGen produces, so that every claim made about the
defense refers to an attack that demonstrably exists on the wire, and not to a synthetic
worst case.

\section{Problem Statement}\label{sec:intro-problem}
The problem this thesis addresses can now be stated. A stream of flows arrives at the
boundary of a protected network, where a \ac{NIDS} is deployed. Part of the traffic is
benign; part belongs to conventional attacks, which the deployed \ac{NIDS} is assumed to
handle, since that is precisely its job; and part has been adversarially manipulated so
that this \ac{NIDS} mishandles it. The defense must decide, for each flow and while the
flow is still in progress, whether it has been manipulated, and, when it has, neutralize
the manipulation before the protected system consumes the flow.

Three constraints qualify an acceptable solution. First, \emph{streaming operation}: the
verdict must be produced packet by packet, as the flow unfolds, because a defense that
waits for a flow to complete has already let the attack through, and buffering whole
flows at a network boundary is not viable performance-wise. Second, \emph{black-box realism}:
the defense may not assume any knowledge the attacker would not grant it, which means it
must be trained and evaluated against realizable, protocol-compliant adversarial
traffic rather than against attack-convenient synthetic perturbations. Third, \emph{realizable
output}: a flow the defense repairs must leave it as valid traffic, packets that parse
and keep their protocol semantics, otherwise the defense has merely moved the
realizability problem from the attacker to the defender.

Two boundaries complete the statement. DEPT complements the protected \ac{NIDS}, it does
not replace it: recognizing the underlying attacks themselves (a flood, a scan, a
brute-force attempt) remains the downstream system's task, and DEPT answers only the
narrower question of whether a flow has been tampered with on its way in. Since the
defense sits inline, its decisions must also fit a real-time latency budget: the per-flow
cost of detection, and of purification when it triggers, has to stay low. This budget
shapes the design choices of \Cref{chap:solution}; measuring the delivered system against
it is left to future work (\Cref{sec:concl-future}).

\section{Work Objectives and Challenges}\label{sec:intro-objectives}
The problem statement was broken into four objectives, which structure both the design
of \Cref{chap:solution} and the research questions that organize the evaluation of
\Cref{chap:evaluation}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{O1 (shared traffic representation).} Find a representation of raw
    traffic that stays close to the packets themselves, so that model outputs can be
    turned back into real traffic, and that architecturally different learners can
    consume at different temporal granularities, from individual packets to aggregated
    flow statistics.
    \item \textbf{O2 (heterogeneous detection).} Build a detection ensemble whose
    members belong to different architectural families, so that a perturbation shaped
    against one member does not automatically transfer to the others, and fuse their
    different opinions into a single per-packet verdict.
    \item \textbf{O3 (input purification).} Learn to reconstruct a flagged flow:
    remove the adversarial manipulation and return the flow to its clean appearance,
    while keeping the result a valid, sendable packet sequence.
    \item \textbf{O4 (streaming-aware evaluation).} Evaluate the system the way it
    would be used: on partial flows as they arrive, with metrics that expose how early
    a verdict becomes reliable, rather than end-of-flow accuracy alone.
\end{itemize}

The setting makes these objectives harder than they may look, and three difficulties
deserve to be named early. The first is imbalance: public attack corpora are dominated
by high-rate floods, so a handful of traffic sources contributes the overwhelming
majority of flows, and both training and evaluation have to be designed so that these
sources neither drown out the rest nor flatter the aggregate numbers. The second is the
thinness of the signal: a manipulation confined to packet timing on a high-rate flood
barely moves any observable feature, and no detector can find a footprint where almost
none exists; \Cref{chap:evaluation} quantifies this limit openly instead of averaging it
away. The third is that the defense inherits the attacker's constraint: purification
edits traffic, and edited traffic must remain realizable, which rules out treating flows
as feature vectors and directly forces the representation choices of O1.
Cutting across all three runs the latency constraint: whatever the pipeline does, it
must do at a streaming pace.

\section{Rationale and Contributions}\label{sec:intro-contributions}
The design of DEPT rests on two decisions, whose full justification occupies
\Cref{chap:back,chap:related} but whose logic can be given in a few lines. The first
concerns detection. A black-box attacker's practical weapon is transferability:
perturbations crafted against a substitute model tend to carry over to the target when
both expose similar, differentiable decision surfaces~\cite{papernot2017practical}.
DEPT's detector therefore stacks two learners from different architectural families, a
\ac{RF}, whose decision function offers no gradient at all, and a \ac{TCN}, which reads
the raw packet sequence, fused at every packet by a small attention-based
meta-learner~\cite{wolpert1992stacked}. What moves one member's verdict does not
necessarily move the other's, and a single gradient signal cannot cross both.

The second decision concerns what to do with a flagged flow. Dropping it would turn
every false positive into denial of service for a legitimate client, so DEPT purifies
instead, in the input-transformation tradition started by
Defense-GAN~\cite{samangouei2018defense}. The purifier is a causal Transformer that
predicts a residual correction over the packet sequence, rather than a \ac{GAN} or a
diffusion model: flows are sequences, which is what self-attention models well;
causality preserves the streaming property, and a residual head makes ``change nothing'' the
default behaviour.

Against this rationale, the thesis makes five contributions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A streaming packet-to-tensor pipeline that turns raw captures into a masked,
    bit-level representation of each flow, emitted at two temporal granularities, so
    that architecturally different learners share one view of the
    same traffic (\Cref{sec:sol-pipeline}).
    \item A heterogeneous detection ensemble, an \ac{RF} and a \ac{TCN} fused per packet
    by an attention meta-learner, together with a streaming comparison
    that scores every model by how early, and not only how well, it separates
    manipulated from clean flows (\Cref{sec:sol-ensemble,sec:eval-meta}).
    \item A causal purification Transformer coupled to an explicit realization step:
    flagged flows are corrected in feature space, written back into protocol-compliant
    packets, and validated with a standard protocol dissector (\texttt{tshark}). On the
    sources where the manipulation leaves a footprint the protected detector can see,
    purification recovers most of the detection the attack destroyed, confirmed not
    through DEPT's own detector but through the \emph{independent} NIDS each attack was
    built to evade (\Cref{sec:sol-reconstructor,sec:eval-purification}).
    \item An end-to-end deployment baseline that measures DEPT as what it is, a defense
    placed in front of a protected NIDS: the full pipeline is run
    over clean, TANTGen-perturbed, and DEPT-defended traffic, and each is scored through
    the real NIDS the attack targets, Kitsune for the Kitsune-native attacks and TANTGen's
    ML-NIDS for the CIC attacks, reporting the attack-success rate the defense removes and
    the detection it restores (\Cref{sec:eval-e2e}).
    \item An adversarial evaluation corpus and its honest accounting: ten
    traffic sources drawn from three public datasets
    (Kitsune, CIC-IDS2017, and CIC-DDoS-2019~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune,cicids2017,sharafaldin2019cicddos}),
    each manipulated by TANTGen in three perturbation modes, evaluated end to end with
    an explicit per-source analysis of where the defense works, where it is inert, and
    why (\Cref{sec:sol-pipeline} and \Cref{chap:evaluation}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Organization of the Document}\label{sec:intro-organization}
The remainder of this document is organized as follows. \Cref{chap:back} presents the
background: learning-based intrusion detection and traffic representation, adversarial
machine learning and the realizability constraints that distinguish it in the network
domain, and the ensemble concepts behind DEPT's detector. \Cref{chap:related} surveys
related work on the attack and the defense sides, treats TANTGen in the detail its role
here requires, and states the gap DEPT fills. \Cref{chap:solution} presents DEPT itself:
the threat model, the system overview, the corpus and packet-to-tensor pipeline, the
detection ensemble, and the purification and realization stages. \Cref{chap:evaluation}
evaluates the delivered system against two research questions, reporting the cases where
it does poorly with the same weight as those where it excels. \Cref{chap:conclusion}
closes with the answers to the research questions, the system's limitations, and future
work.
