\label{chap:solution}

This chapter presents DEPT (Defense via Ensemble and Purification Transformer), the
system this thesis proposes and builds. DEPT composes the three pillars introduced in
\Cref{chap:back} into a single streaming defense: two traffic representations
emitted at different rates, a heterogeneous detection ensemble fused by an attention
meta-learner, and a causal purification Transformer whose output is realized back into
valid packets. The chapter is organized as follows.
\Cref{sec:sol-threat} fixes the threat model, since later decisions are
justified relative to it. \Cref{sec:sol-overview} gives the end-to-end view of the
architecture and its two operating phases. \Cref{sec:sol-pipeline} describes how the
training corpus is constructed and how raw captures become the tensors the models
consume. \Cref{sec:sol-ensemble,sec:sol-reconstructor} then detail the two functional
stages, detection and purification, and \Cref{sec:sol-tools} closes with the
implementation. Design decisions and their rationale belong to this chapter; the
measured behaviour of the resulting system is the subject of \Cref{chap:evaluation}.

\section{Threat Model}\label{sec:sol-threat}
Three kinds of traffic are distinguished. \emph{Benign traffic} is the normal traffic of
a network, with no attack in it. \emph{Attack traffic} belongs to a conventional attack,
such as a flood or a scan, of the kind a \ac{NIDS} is built to detect.
\emph{Adversarial traffic} is attack traffic that has been perturbed so that the
\ac{NIDS} no longer detects it; these perturbations are what DEPT defends against.

The scenario, illustrated in \Cref{fig:sol-context}, involves three parties. A
\emph{protected \ac{NIDS}} guards a network and is assumed to handle benign and attack
traffic correctly, but not adversarial traffic; this is the detector an adversary wants
to get past. An \emph{attacker} produces adversarial traffic by manipulating an ongoing
attack, the evasion attack of \Cref{sec:bg-aml}. \emph{DEPT} sits inline, immediately
upstream of the protected \ac{NIDS}: its job is to decide, packet by packet, whether a
flow is adversarial, and to neutralize the manipulation before the protected system
ever consumes the flow. Since DEPT only separates adversarial traffic from the rest, the
label convention of \Cref{sec:sol-pipeline} groups benign and attack traffic together
as \emph{benign}, in the sense of unmanipulated.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includesvg[width=0.98\textwidth]{./Images/dept_context}
    \caption[DEPT's deployment context.]{DEPT's deployment context. Benign and
    manipulated traffic (generated by TANTGen in this work) arrive interleaved. Flows
    judged clean are forwarded unchanged, and flagged flows pass through the purifier
    first, so the protected NIDS receives traffic whose manipulation has been undone.}
    \label{fig:sol-context}
\end{figure}

The attacker is any adversary with the capabilities below; in this work it is
TANTGen~\cite{pissarra2026tantgen} (\Cref{subsec:rel-tantgen}), which ties them to an
attack that has been demonstrated rather than assumed.
It works in the \emph{black-box} setting of \Cref{subsec:bg-threat-taxonomy}: it has no
access to the architecture, parameters, or gradients of DEPT or of the protected
\ac{NIDS}, and can at most observe detection outcomes. It perturbs two quantities of
live attack traffic, packet timing (inter-arrival times) and size (payload padding),
alone or together (TANTGen's \texttt{iat}, \texttt{size}, and \texttt{both} modes). Its
packets are protocol-compliant. Attacks that break protocol semantics, such as a
wrong checksum or a negative inter-arrival time, are out of scope: they are not working
evasions (\Cref{subsec:bg-realizability}), and stateless checks reject them before any
learned model is involved.

TANTGen takes its gradients from its own surrogate \ac{NIDS}, never from DEPT, so it
cannot test the claim behind the ensemble, which is a claim about gradient paths. A
second adversary, evaluated in \Cref{sec:eval-gradablation}, therefore has white-box
access to the \ac{TCN} (architecture, weights and gradients) and nothing for the Random
Forest, which has no gradient to leak. Its perturbations are protocol-compliant and
\emph{increase-only}, since packets on the wire can be padded or delayed but not
un-sent, and its budget is counted in bytes of padding and seconds of delay.

DEPT, in turn, does not use TANTGen's model, parameters, or surrogate \ac{NIDS}, only
its traffic: labelled flows train the detectors, and flows paired with their clean
originals train the reconstructor (\Cref{subsec:sol-rec-training}), so any generator with
the capabilities above could replace TANTGen. It is not black-box all the way through,
however, since realization writes back only the two quantities the attacker perturbs
(\Cref{subsec:sol-realization}). DEPT thus knows the
attacker's feature set but not its model, the gray-box setting of
\Cref{subsec:bg-threat-taxonomy} seen from the defender's side. Training and test flows
come from the same generator, so DEPT is evaluated against the attacker it was trained
on, and other generators are left to future work (\Cref{sec:concl-future}). Only the
second adversary's added perturbations are never seen in training.

Three non-goals complete the model. DEPT does not defend against \emph{poisoning}: its
training data comes from known sources and the training pipeline is trusted. No claim
is made against an adversary with gradient access to every component, which for the
Random Forest is impossible. And DEPT does not replace the protected \ac{NIDS}:
detecting the underlying attacks (a flood, a scan) stays the \ac{NIDS}'s job, and DEPT
only answers whether a flow has been manipulated, which is what the label convention of
\Cref{sec:sol-pipeline} encodes.

\section{System Overview}\label{sec:sol-overview}
\Cref{fig:sol-architecture} shows the complete architecture. Traffic enters through the
\emph{Packet-to-Tensor pipeline}, which derives two views of each flow
(\Cref{sec:sol-pipeline}) at two rates: a dense stream of masked nPrint per-packet
tokens and a sparse stream of statistics measured on the packets themselves. The two base learners
consume one stream each. The \ac{TCN} produces a verdict logit at every packet, from
the very first one; the \ac{RF} produces a logit at packet~2 and then every tenth
packet, on the aggregated prefix. A per-packet \emph{fusion token} joins the two
prediction streams with a small amount of context, and the \emph{meta-learner}, a
compact causal Transformer, turns that token stream into a per-packet probability that
the flow is adversarial. Flows that never cross the decision threshold are forwarded
untouched. Flows that do are handed to the \emph{reconstructor}, a causal purification
Transformer that undoes the manipulation in feature space, and a \emph{realization} step
then writes that correction back onto the traffic space. Realization is the last
stage of the inference path: what reaches the protected \ac{NIDS} is the realized
traffic, not the purified tensors.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includesvg[width=\textwidth]{./Images/dept_architecture}
    \caption[The DEPT architecture.]{The DEPT architecture. The Packet-to-Tensor
    pipeline feeds two representations of the same flow to two architecturally
    different base learners; their prediction streams are joined into 13-dimensional
    per-packet fusion tokens and fused by a causal mini-Transformer into a streaming
    verdict. Only flagged flows pay the cost of purification.}
    \label{fig:sol-architecture}
\end{figure}

The shape of the system follows from the objectives stated in
\Cref{sec:intro-objectives}. The dual representation answers O1: both learners see the
same flow through a behaviour-focused, identity-free view, each at the granularity it
suits. The ensemble answers O2: the two base learners differ in architecture
(non-differentiable trees against a differentiable network), in input (aggregated
statistics against raw sequences), and in timing (sparse against dense), which is the
diversity that the transferability argument of \Cref{sec:bg-ensembles} asks for. The
reconstructor and the realization step together answer O3, and the per-packet verdicts,
rather than end-of-flow ones, are what O4's evaluation measures.

DEPT operates in two phases. In the \emph{training} phase, all components are fitted
offline, in dependency order. The pipeline first tensorizes every source once. The two
base learners are then trained independently on the training split. The meta-learner is
trained next, on the validation split rather than on the training split, for the reason
given in \Cref{subsec:sol-meta}. The reconstructor is trained last, on matched pairs of
adversarial and clean flows. In the \emph{deployment} phase the flow of
\Cref{fig:sol-architecture} would run continuously: tensorize each arriving packet, run
through both base learners, fuse, and decide. Purification is gated on the ensemble's
flag, so the common case, unflagged clean traffic, never pays for it, and only the flows
the detector marks adversarial are handed to the reconstructor and then realized back
into packets before reaching the protected NIDS.

\section{Dataset Construction and Packet-to-Tensor Pipeline}\label{sec:sol-pipeline}

\paragraph{Traffic corpus.}
The corpus pairs ten traffic sources with adversarial counterparts generated by
TANTGen. Five sources carry over from the datasets most used in the related literature:
the Mirai botnet and SSDP flood captures of Kitsune~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune}, and the
\ac{DDoS}, port scan, and FTP/SSH brute-force captures of
CIC-IDS2017~\cite{sharafaldin2018cicids}. Five more come from CIC-DDoS-2019: four
reflection and amplification floods (NTP, DNS, SSDP, and LDAP) and a SYN
flood~\cite{sharafaldin2019cicddos}. For each source, TANTGen produced one adversarial
variant per perturbation mode (\texttt{both}, \texttt{iat}, \texttt{size}), giving
thirty adversarial source--mode combinations. Two construction decisions should be mentioned.
The CIC-DDoS-2019 captures are attack-dominated from their first seconds and offer no
clean time window to filter on, so each is truncated to its first million packets
instead, the same scale at which the generator caps its own malicious input. And no
source without a TANTGen counterpart was admitted into the corpus: benign flows from
unpaired captures would inflate the benign class without contributing any adversarial
signal to learn against.

\paragraph{Label convention.}
Every flow taken from the raw datasets is labelled \emph{benign}, including their
native attacks, and every TANTGen-generated flow is labelled \emph{adversarial}. This
follows directly from the threat model: detecting a flood or a scan is the protected
\ac{NIDS}'s responsibility, while DEPT's task is to recognize whether a flow has been
\emph{manipulated}. The labels therefore encode the question the system must
answer, ``did the generator touch this flow''. Throughout the rest of this document,
\emph{benign} is used in this sense of \emph{unmanipulated}: the ten original sources are
called benign even though they are drawn from attack captures. One consequence is that,
on a source whose features TANTGen barely moves, the two classes are nearly
indistinguishable for any detector; \Cref{sec:eval-discussion} analyses this cost.

\paragraph{From packets to tensors.}
Each capture is processed in a single pass. Packets are grouped into flows by their
canonical five-tuple and kept in timestamp order, and each packet is encoded
into a $1090$-dimensional token (\Cref{fig:bg-nprint}). Following nPrint
(\Cref{subsec:bg-features}), DEPT encodes the IPv4, TCP, \ac{UDP}, and ICMP headers,
producing a $1088$-bit vector ($480 + 480 + 64 + 64$), and appends two derived
per-packet scalars, the z-scored \ac{IPAT} and a direction bit (the normalization
statistics are computed at tensorization time and stored alongside the tensors, so that
training and inference standardize identically). The per-flow token sequences are
stored as one large ragged array with an offsets table, which keeps loading cheap and
lets every downstream component share the same on-disk artifacts.

The header bits include the fields that identify the particular hosts and ports of a
capture: the source and destination IP addresses, the transport ports, and the IP
identification field. These are the fields behind the memorization shortcut described
in \Cref{subsec:bg-features}, so DEPT \emph{masks} them, zeroing the $144$ bits that
encode them (\Cref{fig:bg-nprint}). Masking forces the detectors to rely on behavioural
structure, such as packet sizing and timing, rather than on the identity of the hosts
of one capture.

The same pass also produces the sparse representation for the \ac{RF}, measured on the
packets themselves rather than derived from the tokens (\Cref{tab:bg-streams}). At each
checkpoint, six base features (inter-arrival time, packet size, payload length, payload
entropy, TCP flags, and direction) are summarized by their mean, standard deviation,
minimum, and maximum over the whole prefix seen so far, a $24$-dimensional vector.
None of the six encodes host or port identity, so this stream needs no masking of its own.
These statistics are defined \emph{incrementally}, by Welford's online algorithm for
means and variances together with running extremes, so that a flow never needs to be
re-buffered: the cost per packet is constant. Checkpoints fire at packet~$2$ and
then every tenth packet. The early checkpoint is deliberate: some attacks generate
extremely short flows. A port scan, for example, averages roughly two packets per flow,
typically a single SYN and its RST, so without the checkpoint at packet~$2$ the forest
would never issue a verdict for precisely the flows that a scan consists of.

The two streams also differ in content and not only in rate: nPrint encodes headers
only, so the payload entropy the forest keys on is structurally invisible to the
\ac{TCN}. Feeding one learner a dense per-packet sequence and the other a sparse,
aggregated summary of a partly different view is also a source of the ensemble diversity
that \Cref{sec:bg-ensembles} argues to be valuable. \Cref{fig:sol-cadence} lays the
resulting prediction pattern out on the packet axis, including the carry-forward
behaviour that the fusion stage of \Cref{subsec:sol-meta} builds on.

\paragraph{Splits and class balance.}
All models share a single $70/15/15$ train/validation/test split, drawn with seed $42$.
The split is made at flow level: every row derived from a flow, be it a packet token, a
checkpoint vector, or a reconstruction pair, lands on the same side, since splitting
per row would leak near-duplicate prefixes of the same flow across sets. It is also
\emph{stratified by source}, so each split preserves the corpus's source mixture, and
it is equally reused by the \ac{RF}, the \ac{TCN}, the meta-learner, and the
reconstructor. This shared split is what later makes their comparison
like-for-like, and it guarantees that the meta-learner, which consumes
the base learners' outputs, is never evaluated on flows any base learner trained on.
Class imbalance is handled by the learners themselves, with balanced class weighting
in both the forest and the neural losses.

\section{Primary Detection: Heterogeneous Stacking Ensemble}\label{sec:sol-ensemble}
The detection stage instantiates the stacking pattern of \Cref{sec:bg-ensembles}: two
deliberately opposite base learners and a learned combiner. The two learners, an \ac{RF}
and a \ac{TCN}, are heterogeneous in architecture and asynchronous in operation: the
network emits a dense verdict on every packet, while the forest emits a sparse verdict
only at its checkpoints, starting at packet~$2$. This is the setting stacking was
designed for, and the reason DEPT fuses them with a learned meta-learner rather than a
fixed rule such as averaging or majority voting (\Cref{subsec:bg-ensemble-types}). The
base learners are described first, the fusion afterwards.

\subsection{Random Forest Base Learner}\label{subsec:sol-rf}
The \ac{RF} consumes the $24$-dimensional checkpoint vectors and answers, at every
checkpoint, whether the prefix statistics look manipulated. The choice of a forest for
this role is doubly motivated. Practically, forests are strong learners for small
tabular inputs and require neither feature scaling nor much tuning
(\Cref{subsec:bg-architectures}). Strategically, a forest's decision function is
piecewise constant and non-differentiable, which makes it the gradient-free member that
the transferability argument of \Cref{subsec:bg-gradient-masking} requires: the forest's
verdicts give no smooth loss surface, even to a surrogate-based attacker.

The configuration was selected by grid search on the validation split, over
$\{200, 400\}$ trees, maximum depths $\{\text{unbounded}, 20, 40\}$, and minimum leaf
sizes $\{1, 2, 5\}$, with $\sqrt{d}$ feature sampling and the \texttt{balanced\_subsample}
class weighting fixed throughout. The selected model uses $200$ trees of maximum depth
$20$ with at least $5$ samples per leaf. The search favoured the \emph{most} regularized
corner of the grid: with only $24$ input dimensions,
deeper, unconstrained trees mostly memorize checkpoint idiosyncrasies, while a bounded
depth and a non-trivial leaf size force better generalization across flows. At
inference time the forest is queried at the pipeline's checkpoints, so its
verdict stream is sparse, delayed by design, and at the cadence of
\Cref{fig:sol-cadence}.

\subsection{Temporal Convolutional Network Base Learner}\label{subsec:sol-tcn}
The \ac{TCN} is the dense half of the ensemble. It consumes the full per-packet token
stream and emits a logit pair at every position, so a flow acquires its first verdict
with its first packet. The network instantiates the architecture of
\Cref{subsec:bg-architectures}: three residual temporal blocks of $128$ channels each,
with two convolutions per block, kernel size $k = 3$, dilations $1$, $2$, and $4$, and
dropout of $0.15$, closed by a per-timestep linear head; in total about $0.8$~million
parameters. Causal padding keeps every output independent of future packets, which is
the property that makes the model legitimate for streaming use, and the dilation schema
gives a receptive field of
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{RF} \;=\; 1 + 2\,(k-1)\sum_{i=0}^{2} 2^{\,i}
              \;=\; 1 + 2\cdot 2\cdot(1+2+4) \;=\; 29 \text{ packets},
    \label{eq:bg-rf}
\end{equation}
so a verdict at any packet integrates evidence from that packet and the $28$ preceding it
(\Cref{fig:bg-tcn}), enough context to cover several \ac{RF} checkpoint intervals (of
roughly $10$) without inflating the parameter count.

Training minimizes per-packet cross-entropy with balanced class weights, averaged
\emph{within} each flow before averaging across flows, so that a ten-thousand-packet
flood does not drown out two-packet scans in the gradient. Sequences are truncated at
$200$ packets when batching, so that a single long flow does not force the whole padded
batch to its length; truncation
therefore discards supervision beyond packet~$200$ rather than distorting it, which is
consistent with the early-detection goal. Optimization
uses AdamW with a cosine learning-rate schedule and early stopping on the validation
split.
The intended division of labour inside the ensemble is thus clear: the \ac{TCN}
contributes an early, dense, but noisier signal, the forest a delayed, sparse, more
accurate one, and neither is expected to be sufficient alone.

\subsection{Meta-Learner (the ``Mini-Transformer'')}\label{subsec:sol-meta}
The meta-learner reconciles the two prediction streams. The difficulty is not accuracy
but \emph{asynchrony}: at most packets the forest has no fresh opinion, and before
packet~$2$ it has none at all, so a fixed rule such as averaging is not even
well-defined at every position. DEPT's answer is a small decoder-only Transformer
over per-packet \emph{fusion tokens}, whose $13$ dimensions are listed in
\Cref{tab:sol-token}. The token gives the model the two base logits, the information
needed to interpret them (how stale the forest's logit is, whether it was refreshed at
this very packet), and a deliberately compact summary of the current packet.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption[The 13-dimensional fusion token.]{The 13-dimensional per-packet fusion
    token consumed by the meta-learner.}
    \label{tab:sol-token}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}cll@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Slot} & \textbf{Feature} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
        \midrule
        0 & RF logit & last checkpoint logit, carried forward; placeholder before packet 2 \\
        1 & TCN logit & dense per-packet logit \\
        2 & \texttt{rf\_age} & packets since the last RF refresh, clipped at $30$; $30$ before packet 2 \\
        3 & \texttt{is\_rf\_checkpoint} & $1$ iff the RF logit was refreshed at this packet \\
        4 & IPAT & z-scored inter-packet arrival time \\
        5 & direction & direction bit of the current packet \\
        6 & packet length & IPv4 total length, normalized by $1500$ \\
        7--12 & TCP flags & URG, ACK, PSH, RST, SYN, FIN bits \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Three design decisions define this component. First, the input is kept minimal on
purpose: the meta-learner is a combiner of the base learners, not another feature
extractor, and
withholding the full $1088$-bit representation forces it to combine the base learners
instead of bypassing them; it also keeps the model at roughly $68$k parameters (two
causal blocks, four heads, $d_{\text{model}}=64$), negligible next to either base.
Second, the pre-checkpoint gap is filled by a \emph{learnable} placeholder logit rather
than an imputed constant. A zero would be read as a genuine ``undecided'' forest
opinion; a trainable parameter lets the model learn its own prior for ``the forest has
not spoken yet''. Third, attention is causally masked and positions are encoded with
the standard sinusoidal scheme, so the fused verdict at packet $t$ depends only on
packets up to $t$, preserving the streaming property end to end.

The training discipline matters as much as the architecture. Fitting the meta-learner on
the same data its base learners trained on would let it learn their \emph{overfit}
confidence rather than their generalizing behaviour~\cite{wolpert1992stacked}, so the
meta-learner trains on the validation split, where both base learners' predictions are
out-of-fold, and that split is sub-divided (seed $43$) into a meta-train and a meta-val
part, the latter using early stopping on streaming \ac{AUROC} at packet $10$. The
loss is the same balanced per-packet cross-entropy as the \ac{TCN}'s, plus an auxiliary
``beat the best base'' term (weight $0.5$) that penalizes, at each packet, any
shortfall of the meta-learner's true-class confidence against the better of the two
base learners. The intent is to make ``never worse than the best base'' an explicit
training signal; what this loss does and does not achieve in practice is reported with
the ablations in \Cref{sec:eval-meta}. At deployment the meta-learner's per-packet
probability is compared against a fixed threshold of $0.5$; a flow is flagged the first
time its score crosses it, which is also the event the earliest-detection metric of
\Cref{chap:evaluation} uses.

\section{Input Purification: Reconstructor}\label{sec:sol-reconstructor}
Detection alone would leave only a blunt response to a flagged flow, dropping it, which
turns every false positive into a denial of service for a legitimate client. DEPT's
second stage takes the purification route of \Cref{subsec:bg-defense-paradigms}
instead: undo the manipulation and let the repaired flow proceed. The reconstructor is a
causal Transformer denoiser operating directly on the per-packet token sequence, trained
to map an adversarial flow onto its clean counterpart (\Cref{fig:sol-purifier}). It is
therefore not a generative purifier in the sense of \Cref{subsec:bg-defense-paradigms}:
it models no distribution over benign traffic and samples nothing, but regresses the
correction directly.

\subsection{Purification Model}\label{subsec:sol-rec-model}
The reconstructor shares the meta-learner's scaffold, the decoder-only block of
\Cref{fig:bg-transformer} in its pre-norm form, with a strict causal mask and
sinusoidal positions, scaled up to three layers, four
heads, and $d_{\text{model}}=128$ (about $678$K parameters), with the $1090$-dimensional
token as both input and output. Its head, however, does not predict the entire clean sequence
directly: it predicts a per-dimension \emph{residual} $\Delta$, and the output is the
input plus $\Delta$ (\Cref{fig:sol-purifier-a}). This residual formulation fits the
problem. An adversarial example is a perturbation of a clean sample, and the
manipulations of this threat model touch only two physical quantities, timing and size, so out of the
$1090$ dimensions of a token only a handful actually need correcting; with a residual
head, the identity map is the easy default ($\Delta \approx 0$), the untouched
$\sim\!1050$ header bits are preserved, and the model's capacity is spent on
the localized corrections that matter. The causal mask, in turn, keeps the purifier
valid for streaming use and compatible with key--value caching
(\Cref{subsec:bg-architectures}). The purifier is nonetheless not autoregressive, since it
never feeds its own output back as input: it corrects every input position in a single
pass, each from packets $\leq t$ alone, so the causal mask is there for streaming, not
for generation.

\subsection{Training on Matched Flow Pairs}\label{subsec:sol-rec-training}
Purification is learned from \emph{matched pairs}. Because the attacker perturbs a flow
without changing its five-tuple identity, an adversarial flow can be aligned with the
clean flow it was derived from by matching canonical five-tuple keys. In practice, TANTGen also
splits, merges, and drops flows, so only about a third of the adversarial flows find
such a counterpart (\Cref{subsec:eval-datasets}); these form the (adversarial
$\rightarrow$ clean) pairs on which reconstruction is supervised. Pairs shorter than two packets are dropped,
since a single packet carries no inter-arrival interval and thus no timing signal to
restore. Pairs follow the same seed-$42$ flow-level split as everything else, keeping
the test pairs unseen by every base learner.

The loss is a smooth-L1 distance between the reconstructed and the clean sequence,
masked over padding and averaged within each flow before averaging across flows, the
same length-normalization argument as in \Cref{subsec:sol-tcn}; smooth-L1 is preferred
over a plain squared error for its robustness to the occasional large deviation.
One further choice is important: the loss weights all $1090$ dimensions
\emph{uniformly}. This means that the training signal contains no hint of where TANTGen's perturbation
lives, so the model must discover for itself which dimensions are trustworthy and
which have been tampered with. This keeps the purification model
\emph{attack-agnostic}: nothing in its objective is specific to TANTGen's footprint, even
though that footprint is known here. During development a variant
weighted towards the known perturbed dimensions was also explored, and it was dropped
from the final design because it ties the defense to one attacker's
signature. This agnosticism is a property of this specific feature-space stage, not of the whole
purification path, and \Cref{subsec:sol-realization} states where it stops.

\subsection{Traffic-Space Realization and Validation}\label{subsec:sol-realization}
A purifier that edits traffic inherits the realizability obligation of
\Cref{subsec:bg-realizability}: its output must be traffic that can exist on the wire.
DEPT meets this obligation with an explicit \emph{realization} step
(\Cref{fig:sol-purifier-b}) at the end of the inference path. Rebuilding packets from
scratch out of the purified tensors would be impossible, as nPrint encodes only headers and the
identity fields are masked to zero. Realization therefore works by \emph{overlay}: we
keep each original packet, with its identity and payload content intact,
and write back only the two quantities purification may have changed. The purified
IPv4 total length is decoded from its sixteen header bits and applied by trimming the application payload, which
is the inverse of the attacker's size perturbation; the purified inter-arrival time is
de-normalized and applied to the packet's capture timestamp. The packets are then
re-serialized with scapy, which recomputes the checksums and length fields that depend
on the edit. Alignment between tensor rows and packets reuses the pipeline's canonical
flow grouping, giving a one-to-one correspondence; any flow whose packet count fails to
match its tensor length is skipped, counted, and reported at the end.

The overlay has a price: the deployed pipeline is not attack-agnostic end to end. The
reconstructor of \Cref{subsec:sol-rec-training} corrects all $1090$ dimensions, but
realization writes back exactly two, the axes the threat model of \Cref{sec:sol-threat}
admits. An adversary that perturbed, for example, the \ac{TTL} or the TCP window would
still be flagged and denoised in feature space, but no overlay rule exists for those
fields, so the flow would reach the protected \ac{NIDS} unrepaired and DEPT would
degrade to detection alone. Deriving the fields to edit from the purifier's own residual
instead of fixing them in advance is left as future work (\Cref{sec:concl-future}).

Every realized packet is finally passed through the \texttt{tshark} dissector, and
protocol compliance is scored as the fraction of packets parsed without a
malformed-packet complaint. This check is the one part of the stage that stays off the
inference path: it is a measurement, for the compliance figures of
\Cref{sec:eval-purification} and for forensics, not a runtime filter, so no packet waits
on it. One caveat should be stated here: for
application-layer \emph{text} protocols, such as the SSDP \texttt{M-SEARCH} messages
that dominate one source, a size edit necessarily corrupts the L7 grammar
even when all IP and UDP fields remain consistent, so such packets cannot be faithfully
realized by overlay at all. They are reported separately rather than scored as
compliance failures, and \Cref{sec:eval-purification} gives the full accounting.

Finally, since the realized packets are what the protected \ac{NIDS} receives,
purification is judged mainly through that \ac{NIDS} rather than through DEPT's own
detector: Kitsune~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune} for the two Kitsune-native sources and
TANTGen's own ML-NIDS flow classifiers for the eight CIC attacks, in every case the
detector TANTGen calibrated that attack's evasion against
(\Cref{subsec:eval-e2e-detect}).

\section{Implementation and Tools}\label{sec:sol-tools}
DEPT is implemented in Python. The three sequence models (\ac{TCN}, meta-learner,
reconstructor) are built in PyTorch and the \ac{RF} in scikit-learn. The nPrint
encoding is reimplemented in Python on top of scapy, following the bit layout of the
original tool~\cite{holland2021nprint}; writing the encoding into the pipeline avoids an
external binary, lets identity masking happen inline, and keeps tensorization a
single pass over each capture. Scapy also drives packet parsing and the realization
step, while \texttt{tshark}, \texttt{editcap}, and \texttt{tcpdump} handle dissection,
time-window filtering of CIC-IDS2017, and the packet-capping of the large CIC-DDoS-2019
captures, respectively.

The system is organized as four model stages behind five configuration files, one per
stage plus one for the pipeline, with the three evaluation studies of
\Cref{chap:evaluation} (the gradient-guided attack, the deployment baseline, and the
comparison with a published purifier) adding one configuration file each in the same
pattern; every file fully determines its stage's
data scope, architecture, and training settings, and a small runner script per stage
executes it end to end. All stages read the same tensor artifacts and the same stored split, which is
how the shared-split principle of \Cref{sec:sol-pipeline} is enforced in practice,
and derived artifacts are cached. \Cref{tab:sol-hyper}
summarizes all four learned components. The source layout mirrors the stages, with the
feature pipeline under \texttt{src/feature\_extraction}, the models under
\texttt{src/models}, per-stage entry points under \texttt{scripts}, and configuration
under \texttt{config}; further operational details live in the repository rather
than in this document.

With the architecture in place, what remains is to show that it meets its goals: that
the ensemble detects manipulation early, and that purification actually returns
flagged flows to the benign manifold while emitting valid packets. These are the
questions \Cref{chap:evaluation} takes up.
