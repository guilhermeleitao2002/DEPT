\fancychapter{Solution: DEPT Architecture}

\label{chap:solution}

This chapter presents DEPT (Defense via Ensemble and Purification Transformer), the
system this thesis proposes and builds. DEPT composes the three pillars introduced in
\Cref{chap:back} into a single streaming defense: a bit-level traffic representation
emitted at two rates, a heterogeneous detection ensemble fused by an attention
meta-learner, and a causal purification Transformer whose output is realized back into
valid packets. The chapter is organized as a designer would proceed.
\Cref{sec:sol-threat} fixes the threat model, since every later decision is only
justified relative to it. \Cref{sec:sol-overview} gives the end-to-end view of the
architecture and its two operating phases. \Cref{sec:sol-pipeline} describes how the
training corpus is constructed and how raw captures become the tensors the models
consume. \Cref{sec:sol-ensemble,sec:sol-reconstructor} then detail the two functional
stages, detection and purification, and \Cref{sec:sol-tools} closes with the
implementation. Design decisions and their rationale belong to this chapter; the
measured behaviour of the resulting system is the subject of \Cref{chap:evaluation}.

\section{Threat Model}\label{sec:sol-threat}
The scenario, illustrated in \Cref{fig:sol-context}, involves three parties. A
\emph{protected \ac{NIDS}} guards a network and is assumed to handle conventional,
non-adversarial attack traffic; this is the detector an adversary wants to get past. An
\emph{attacker} manipulates the traffic of an ongoing attack so that this \ac{NIDS} no
longer recognizes it, in the mould of the evasion attacks of \Cref{sec:bg-aml}.
\emph{DEPT} sits inline, immediately upstream of the protected \ac{NIDS}: its job is to
decide, packet by packet, whether a flow has been adversarially manipulated, and to
neutralize the manipulation before the protected system ever consumes the flow.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includesvg[width=0.98\textwidth]{./Images/dept_context}
    \caption[DEPT's deployment context.]{DEPT's deployment context. Benign traffic and
    adversarially manipulated traffic (generated by TANTGen in this work) arrive
    interleaved. DEPT inspects every flow in streaming fashion: flows judged clean are
    forwarded unchanged, while flagged flows pass through the purifier first, so that
    the protected NIDS downstream receives traffic whose adversarial manipulation has
    been undone.}
    \label{fig:sol-context}
\end{figure}

The attacker is modelled after TANTGen~\cite{pissarra2026tantgen}, the Transformer-based
adversarial traffic generator developed in the sibling thesis and reviewed in depth in
\Cref{chap:related}. This choice pins the threat model to capabilities that have been
demonstrated rather than assumed. The attacker operates in the \emph{black-box} setting
of \Cref{subsec:bg-threat-taxonomy}: it has no access to the architecture, parameters,
or gradients of DEPT or of the protected \ac{NIDS}, and can at most observe detection
outcomes while probing. It perturbs live attack traffic along two physical axes, the
timing of packets (their inter-arrival times) and their size (payload padding), which
TANTGen exposes as three perturbation modes: \texttt{iat} (timing only), \texttt{size}
(size only), and \texttt{both}. Crucially, the generated traffic is protocol-compliant
by construction; it consists of packets that can actually be sent and that a dissector
parses without complaint.

We further adopt a \emph{functional realizability} assumption: attacks that violate
protocol semantics, for instance a broken checksum, a length field inconsistent with
the bytes on the wire, or a negative inter-arrival time, are out of scope by design.
This is a scoping decision rather than a weakness. As discussed in
\Cref{subsec:bg-realizability}, a perturbation that describes no sendable packet is not
a working evasion in the first place, and such malformed traffic is trivially rejected
by stateless checks well before any learned model needs to be involved.

TANTGen fixes the threat model to demonstrated capabilities, but it also bounds what
that model can be asked. Being gradient-free, it cannot price a gradient path, and the
central claim of this design, that fusing a non-differentiable learner with a
differentiable one raises the cost of evasion, is a claim precisely about gradient
paths. Establishing it needs an adversary that has one.

We therefore admit a second, strictly stronger adversary alongside TANTGen, and evaluate
against it in \Cref{sec:eval-gradablation}. It is granted \emph{white-box access to the
\ac{TCN}}: exact architecture, weights and gradients. It is granted nothing at all for
the Random Forest, which is not merely withheld but non-differentiable, so no gradient
exists to leak. This is the sharpest form of the heterogeneity question rather than a
concession: the attacker holds one half of the ensemble exactly and must reach the other
half through the traffic they share. Its perturbations obey the same functional
realizability assumption as TANTGen's and are further constrained to be
\emph{increase-only}, since packets already on the wire may be padded or delayed but
never un-sent, and are measured in the units an attacker actually spends, bytes of
padding and seconds of delay, rather than in an abstract norm.

Three non-goals complete the model. First, DEPT does not defend against
\emph{poisoning}: its training data is curated offline from known sources, and the
training pipeline is trusted. Second, no claim is made against an adversary with
gradient access to \emph{every} component, which for the Random Forest is not a
realistic capability but an impossible one. Third, DEPT does not replace the protected
\ac{NIDS}. Detecting the underlying
attacks themselves (a flood, a scan, a brute-force attempt) remains the downstream
system's job; DEPT answers only the narrower question of whether a flow has been
adversarially tampered with. This division of labour directly shapes the labelling
convention of \Cref{sec:sol-pipeline}.

\section{System Overview}\label{sec:sol-overview}
\Cref{fig:sol-architecture} shows the complete architecture. Traffic enters through the
\emph{Packet-to-Tensor pipeline}, which converts each flow into the masked nPrint
representation of \Cref{subsec:bg-features} and emits it at two rates: a dense
per-packet token stream and a sparse stream of prefix statistics. The two base learners
consume one stream each. The \ac{TCN} produces a verdict logit at every packet, from
the very first one; the \ac{RF} produces a logit at packet~2 and then every ten
packets, on the aggregated prefix. A per-packet \emph{fusion token} joins the two
prediction streams with a small amount of context, and the \emph{meta-learner}, a
compact causal Transformer, turns that token stream into a per-packet probability that
the flow is adversarial. Flows that never cross the decision threshold are forwarded
untouched. Flows that do are handed to the \emph{reconstructor}, a causal purification
Transformer that undoes the manipulation in feature space; its output is passed on to
the protected \ac{NIDS}, and a separate \emph{realization} step can materialize the
purified flow as an actual PCAP whenever concrete packets are required.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includesvg[width=\textwidth]{./Images/dept_architecture}
    \caption[The DEPT architecture.]{The DEPT architecture. The Packet-to-Tensor
    pipeline feeds two representations of the same flow to two architecturally
    different base learners; their prediction streams are joined into 13-dimensional
    per-packet fusion tokens and fused by a causal mini-Transformer into a streaming
    verdict. Only flagged flows pay the cost of purification. Realization (the mapping
    of the purified flow back onto concrete packets, validated with tshark) runs off
    the inference path.}
    \label{fig:sol-architecture}
\end{figure}

The shape of the system follows from the objectives stated in
\Cref{sec:intro-objectives}. The shared representation and its dual emission cadence
answer O1: both learners see the same masked, behaviour-focused view of a flow, each at
the granularity it is suited for. The ensemble answers O2: the two base learners differ
in architecture (non-differentiable trees against a differentiable network), in input
(aggregated statistics against raw sequences), and in timing (sparse against dense),
which is exactly the diversity that the transferability argument of
\Cref{sec:bg-ensembles} asks for. The reconstructor answers O3, restoring flagged flows
to the benign manifold while keeping them realizable. The streaming discipline that
runs through all of it, verdicts at every packet rather than at end of flow, is what
O4's evaluation axis is later measured against.

DEPT operates in two phases. In the \emph{training} phase, all components are fitted
offline, in dependency order. The pipeline first tensorizes every source once. The two
base learners are then trained independently on the training split. The meta-learner is
trained next, not on the training split but on the validation split, where the base
learners' predictions are out-of-fold; \Cref{subsec:sol-meta} returns to why this
matters. The reconstructor is trained last, on matched pairs of adversarial and clean
flows. Its purification \emph{effect} is then quantified primarily against the
\emph{independent} NIDS each attack was built to evade, Kitsune or TANTGen's ML-NIDS
(\Cref{sec:eval-e2e}), with the already-trained \ac{TCN} kept only as a secondary
in-distribution diagnostic; \Cref{sec:eval-purification} discusses why the external
judge, not DEPT's own detector, carries the end-to-end claim. In the \emph{deployment}
phase the flow of \Cref{fig:sol-architecture} runs continuously: tensorize each arriving
packet, update both base learners, fuse, and decide. Purification is gated on the
ensemble's flag, so the common case, clean traffic, never pays for it, and only the
flows the detector marks adversarial are handed to the reconstructor before reaching the
protected NIDS. This detect-then-purify cycle is
the design point on which the real-time requirement of \Cref{sec:intro-problem}
rests; measuring it in wall-clock terms is left to future work
(\Cref{sec:concl-future}).

\section{Dataset Construction and Packet-to-Tensor Pipeline}\label{sec:sol-pipeline}

\paragraph{Traffic corpus.}
The corpus pairs ten benign traffic sources with adversarial counterparts generated by
TANTGen. Five sources carry over from the datasets most used in the related literature:
the Mirai botnet and SSDP flood captures of Kitsune~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune}, and the
\ac{DDoS}, port scan, and FTP/SSH brute-force captures of
CIC-IDS2017~\cite{cicids2017}. Five more come from CIC-DDoS-2019's
reflection and amplification floods (NTP, DNS, SSDP, SYN, and
LDAP)~\cite{sharafaldin2019cicddos}. For each source, TANTGen produced one adversarial
variant per perturbation mode (\texttt{both}, \texttt{iat}, \texttt{size}), giving
thirty adversarial source--mode combinations. Two construction decisions deserve mention.
The CIC-DDoS-2019 captures are attack-dominated from their first seconds and offer no
clean time window to filter on, so each is truncated to its first million packets
instead, the same scale at which the generator caps its own malicious input. And no
source without a TANTGen counterpart was admitted into the corpus: benign flows from
unpaired captures would inflate the benign class without contributing any adversarial
signal to learn against.

\paragraph{Label convention.}
Every flow taken from the raw datasets is labelled \emph{benign}, including their
native attacks, and every TANTGen-generated flow is labelled \emph{adversarial}. This
follows directly from the threat model: detecting a flood or a scan is the protected
\ac{NIDS}'s responsibility, while DEPT's task is to recognize whether a flow has been
\emph{manipulated}. The labels therefore encode precisely the question the system must
answer, ``did the generator touch this flow''. The convention has a measurable
consequence, namely that a source whose features TANTGen barely moves is close to
unlabellable no matter the detector, and \Cref{sec:eval-discussion} analyses this cost
openly rather than folding it into aggregate numbers.

\paragraph{From packets to tensors.}
Each capture is processed in a single pass. Packets are grouped into flows by their
canonical five-tuple and kept in stable timestamp order, and each packet is encoded
into the $1090$-dimensional token of \Cref{subsec:bg-features}: the $1088$ masked
nPrint header bits plus the z-scored \ac{IPAT} and a direction bit (the normalization
statistics are computed at tensorization time and stored alongside the tensors, so that
training and inference standardize identically). The per-flow token sequences are
stored as one large ragged array with an offsets table, which keeps loading cheap and
lets every downstream model share the same on-disk artifacts.

The same pass also produces the sparse representation for the \ac{RF}. At each
checkpoint, six base features (inter-arrival time, packet size, payload length, payload
entropy, TCP flags, and direction) are summarized by their mean, standard deviation,
minimum, and maximum over the whole prefix seen so far, a $24$-dimensional vector.
These statistics are defined \emph{incrementally}, by Welford's online algorithm for
means and variances together with running extrema, so that a flow need never be
buffered: the cost per packet is constant, and the statistics available at a checkpoint
are exactly those the forest would see at the same point of a live flow. Offline, where
the whole capture is already in memory, the same quantities are computed by vectorized
per-flow cumulative sums, which agree with the incremental form to floating-point
round-off and are considerably faster to build; the incremental definition remains the
one a deployed ingress would evaluate per live flow. Checkpoints fire at packet~$2$ and
then every tenth packet, the cadence motivated in \Cref{subsec:bg-features}: without
the early checkpoint, flows shorter than ten packets, which include essentially every
port-scan flow, would end before the forest ever voted.
\Cref{fig:sol-cadence} lays the resulting prediction pattern out on the packet axis,
including the carry-forward behaviour that the fusion stage of
\Cref{subsec:sol-meta} builds on.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includesvg[width=0.98\textwidth]{./Images/dept_cadence}
    \caption[The dual prediction cadence over a flow.]{The dual prediction cadence over
    the first packets of a flow. The TCN (circles) emits a verdict at every packet,
    starting at the first. The Random Forest (squares) emits only at prefix checkpoints
    (packet $2$, then every tenth packet, shaded); between checkpoints its last logit
    is carried forward, and before packet~$2$ it has no verdict at all. The
    \texttt{rf\_age} row shows the recency counter that the fusion token exposes to the
    meta-learner. The meta-learner (diamonds) fuses both streams into a fresh verdict
    at every packet.}
    \label{fig:sol-cadence}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Splits and class balance.}
All models share a single $70/15/15$ train/validation/test split, drawn with seed $42$.
The split is \emph{flow-atomic}: every row derived from a flow, be it a packet token, a
checkpoint vector, or a reconstruction pair, lands on the same side, since splitting
per row would leak near-duplicate prefixes of the same flow across sets. It is also
\emph{stratified by source}, so each split preserves the corpus's source mixture, and
it is reused verbatim by the \ac{RF}, the \ac{TCN}, the meta-learner, and the
reconstructor. This shared discipline is what later makes their comparison
like-for-like, and it guarantees that the meta-learner and reconstructor, which consume
the base learners' outputs, are never evaluated on flows any base learner trained on.
Class imbalance is treated at two levels. Because the reflection floods contribute
vastly more benign flows than the other sources, the benign pool is capped at three
benign flows per adversarial flow before training; the residual imbalance is then
handled by the learners themselves, with balanced class weighting in both the forest
and the neural losses.

\section{Primary Detection: Heterogeneous Stacking Ensemble}\label{sec:sol-ensemble}
The detection stage instantiates the stacking pattern of \Cref{sec:bg-ensembles}: two
deliberately dissimilar base learners and a learned combiner. The base learners are
described first, the fusion afterwards.

\subsection{Random Forest Base Learner}\label{subsec:sol-rf}
The \ac{RF} consumes the $24$-dimensional checkpoint vectors and answers, at every
checkpoint, whether the prefix statistics look manipulated. The choice of a forest for
this role is doubly motivated. Practically, forests are strong learners for small
tabular inputs and need neither feature scaling nor much tuning
(\Cref{subsec:bg-architectures}). Strategically, a forest's decision function is
piecewise constant and non-differentiable, which makes it the gradient-free member that
the transferability argument of \Cref{subsec:bg-gradient-masking} requires: even a
surrogate-based attacker obtains no smooth loss surface that extends to the forest's
verdicts.

The configuration was selected by grid search on the validation split, over
$\{200, 400\}$ trees, maximum depths $\{\text{unbounded}, 20, 40\}$, and minimum leaf
sizes $\{1, 2, 5\}$, with $\sqrt{d}$ feature sampling and the \texttt{balanced\_subsample}
class weighting fixed throughout. The selected model uses $200$ trees of maximum depth
$20$ with at least $5$ samples per leaf. It is worth noting that the search favoured
the \emph{most} regularized corner of the grid: with only $24$ input dimensions,
deeper, unconstrained trees mostly memorize checkpoint idiosyncrasies, while a bounded
depth and a non-trivial leaf size force splits that generalize across flows. At
inference time the forest is queried exactly at the pipeline's checkpoints, so its
verdict stream is sparse, delayed by design, and refreshed at the cadence of
\Cref{fig:sol-cadence}.

\subsection{Temporal Convolutional Network Base Learner}\label{subsec:sol-tcn}
The \ac{TCN} is the dense half of the ensemble. It consumes the full per-packet token
stream and emits a logit pair at every position, so a flow acquires its first verdict
with its first packet. The network instantiates the architecture of
\Cref{subsec:bg-architectures}: three residual temporal blocks of $128$ channels each,
kernel size $3$, dilations $1$, $2$, and $4$, and dropout of $0.15$, closed by a
per-timestep linear head; in total about $0.8$~million parameters. Causal padding keeps
every output independent of future packets, which is the property that makes the model
legitimate for streaming use, and the dilation schedule gives the receptive field of
$29$ packets derived in \Cref{eq:bg-rf}, enough context to cover several \ac{RF}
checkpoint intervals without inflating the parameter count.

Training minimizes per-packet cross-entropy with balanced class weights, averaged
\emph{within} each flow before averaging across flows, so that a ten-thousand-packet
flood does not drown out two-packet scans in the gradient. Sequences are truncated at
$200$ packets during batching to bound the padded batch size; the verdict semantics are
unaffected, since a streaming detector is judged on prefixes anyway. Optimization uses
AdamW with a cosine learning-rate schedule and early stopping on the validation split.
The intended division of labour inside the ensemble is thus clear-cut: the \ac{TCN}
contributes an early, dense, but noisier signal, the forest a delayed, sparse, more
conservative one, and neither is expected to be sufficient alone.

\subsection{Attention-Based Meta-Learner (the ``Mini-Transformer'')}\label{subsec:sol-meta}
The meta-learner reconciles the two prediction streams. The difficulty is not accuracy
but \emph{asynchrony}: at most packets the forest has no fresh opinion, and before
packet~$2$ it has none at all, so a fixed rule such as averaging is not even
well-defined at every position. DEPT's answer is a small causal Transformer encoder
over per-packet \emph{fusion tokens}, whose $13$ dimensions are listed in
\Cref{tab:sol-token}. The token gives the model the two base logits, the bookkeeping
needed to interpret them (how stale the forest's logit is, whether it was refreshed at
this very packet), and a deliberately compact summary of the current packet.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption[The 13-dimensional fusion token.]{The 13-dimensional per-packet fusion
    token consumed by the meta-learner.}
    \label{tab:sol-token}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}cll@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Slot} & \textbf{Feature} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
        \midrule
        0 & RF logit & last checkpoint logit, carried forward; placeholder before packet 2 \\
        1 & TCN logit & dense per-packet logit \\
        2 & \texttt{rf\_age} & packets since the last RF refresh, clipped at $30$ \\
        3 & \texttt{is\_rf\_checkpoint} & $1$ iff the RF logit was refreshed at this packet \\
        4 & IPAT & z-scored inter-packet arrival time \\
        5 & direction & direction bit of the current packet \\
        6 & packet length & IPv4 total length, normalized by $1500$ \\
        7--12 & TCP flags & URG, ACK, PSH, RST, SYN, FIN bits \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Three design decisions define this component. First, the input is kept minimal on
purpose: the meta-learner is a \emph{stacker}, not another feature extractor, and
withholding the full $1088$-bit representation forces it to combine the base learners
instead of bypassing them; it also keeps the model at roughly $68$K parameters (two
encoder layers, four heads, $d_{\text{model}}=64$), negligible next to either base.
Second, the pre-checkpoint gap is filled by a \emph{learnable} placeholder logit rather
than an imputed constant. A zero would be read as a genuine ``undecided'' forest
opinion; a trainable parameter lets the model learn its own prior for ``the forest has
not spoken yet''. Third, attention is causally masked and positions are encoded with
the standard sinusoidal scheme, so the fused verdict at packet $t$ depends only on
packets up to $t$, preserving the streaming property end to end.

The training discipline matters as much as the architecture. Fitting a stacker on the
same data its base learners trained on would let it learn their \emph{overfit}
confidence rather than their generalizing behaviour~\cite{wolpert1992stacked}, so the
meta-learner trains on the validation split, where both base learners' predictions are
out-of-fold, and that split is sub-divided (seed $43$) into a meta-train and a meta-val
part, the latter driving early stopping on streaming \ac{AUROC} at packet $10$. The
loss is the same balanced per-packet cross-entropy as the \ac{TCN}'s, plus an auxiliary
``beat the best base'' term (weight $0.5$) that penalizes, at each packet, any
shortfall of the meta-learner's true-class confidence against the better of the two
base learners. The intent is to make ``never worse than the best base'' an explicit
training signal; what this loss does and does not achieve in practice is reported with
the ablations in \Cref{sec:eval-meta}. At deployment the meta-learner's per-packet
probability is compared against a fixed threshold of $0.5$; a flow is flagged the first
time its score crosses it, which is also the event the earliest-detection metric of
\Cref{chap:evaluation} records.

\section{Input Purification: Reconstructor Transformer}\label{sec:sol-reconstructor}
Detection alone would leave only a blunt response to a flagged flow, dropping it, which
turns every false positive into denial of service for a legitimate client. DEPT's
second stage takes the purification route of \Cref{subsec:bg-defense-paradigms}
instead: undo the manipulation and let the repaired flow proceed. Within the taxonomy
of generative purifiers, the reconstructor is a causal Transformer denoiser operating
directly on the per-packet token sequence, trained to map an adversarial flow onto its
clean counterpart (\Cref{fig:sol-purifier}).

    \hfill

    back onto the original packets, and the result is checked with a dissector.]{
        \includesvg[width=0.44\textwidth]{./Images/dept_realization}
        \label{fig:sol-purifier-b}}
    \caption[The purification stage.]{The purification stage. Purification happens in
    feature space (a); whenever concrete packets are needed, realization (b) overlays
    the two purified quantities onto the original adversarial packets, preserving
    identity and payload content, and recomputes the dependent header fields.}
    \label{fig:sol-purifier}

\subsection{Purification Model}\label{subsec:sol-rec-model}
The reconstructor shares the meta-learner's scaffold, a pre-norm Transformer encoder
under a strict causal mask with sinusoidal positions, scaled up to three layers, four
heads, and $d_{\text{model}}=128$ (about $678$K parameters), with the $1090$-dimensional
token as both input and output. Its head, however, does not predict the clean sequence
directly: it predicts a per-dimension \emph{residual} $\Delta$, and the output is the
input plus $\Delta$. This residual formulation encodes the right prior for the problem.
TANTGen's manipulations touch two physical quantities, timing and size, so out of the
$1090$ dimensions of a token only a handful actually need correcting; with a residual
head, the identity map is the easy default ($\Delta \approx 0$), the untouched
$\sim\!1050$ header bits are preserved for free, and the model's capacity is spent on
the localized corrections that matter. The causal mask, in turn, keeps the purifier
valid for streaming use and, as discussed for decoder-style models in
\Cref{subsec:bg-architectures}, makes it amenable to key--value caching; the present
implementation runs whole-prefix forwards, and \Cref{chap:evaluation} measures it as
built, so caching remains an available engineering optimization rather than a claimed
one.

\subsection{Training on Matched Flow Pairs}\label{subsec:sol-rec-training}
Purification is learned from \emph{matched pairs}. Because TANTGen perturbs a flow
without changing its five-tuple identity, every adversarial flow can be aligned with
the exact clean flow it was derived from by matching canonical five-tuple keys; the
result is a corpus of (adversarial $\rightarrow$ clean) sequence pairs on which
reconstruction is supervised directly. Pairs shorter than two packets are dropped,
since a single packet carries no inter-arrival interval and thus no timing signal to
restore. Pairs follow the same seed-$42$ flow-atomic split as everything else, keeping
the test pairs unseen by every base learner.

The loss is a smooth-L1 distance between the reconstructed and the clean sequence,
masked over padding and averaged within each flow before averaging across flows, the
same length-normalization argument as in \Cref{subsec:sol-tcn}; smooth-L1 is preferred
over a plain squared error for its robustness to the occasional large deviation.
One further choice deserves emphasis: the loss weights all $1090$ dimensions
\emph{uniformly}. The training signal contains no hint of where TANTGen's perturbation
lives, so the model must discover for itself which dimensions are trustworthy and
which have been tampered with. This keeps the purifier \emph{attack-agnostic}: nothing
in its objective is specific to TANTGen's footprint. During development a variant
weighted towards the known perturbed dimensions was also explored, and it was dropped
from the final design precisely because it ties the defense to one attacker's
signature; the honest, deployable configuration is the uniform one.

\subsection{Traffic-Space Realization and Validation}\label{subsec:sol-realization}
A purifier that edits traffic inherits the realizability obligation of
\Cref{subsec:bg-realizability}: its output must be traffic that can exist on the wire.
DEPT discharges this obligation with an explicit \emph{realization} step
(\Cref{fig:sol-purifier-b}). Rebuilding packets from scratch out of the purified
tensors would be both impossible and unwise, as nPrint encodes only headers and the
identity fields are masked to zero. Realization therefore works by \emph{overlay}: it
keeps each original adversarial packet, with its identity and payload content intact,
and writes back only the two quantities purification may have changed. The purified
IPv4 total length is decoded from its sixteen header bits (rounded back to the
tri-state alphabet) and applied by trimming or padding the application payload, which
is the inverse of TANTGen's size perturbation; the purified inter-arrival time is
de-normalized and applied to the packet's capture timestamp. The packets are then
re-serialized with scapy, which recomputes the checksums and length fields that depend
on the edit. Alignment between tensor rows and packets reuses the pipeline's canonical
flow grouping, giving a one-to-one correspondence; any flow whose packet count fails to
match its tensor length is skipped and counted, never silently mangled.

Every realized packet is finally passed through the \texttt{tshark} dissector, and
protocol compliance is scored as the fraction of packets parsed without a
malformed-packet complaint. One structural caveat is worth stating already here: for
application-layer \emph{text} protocols, such as the SSDP \texttt{M-SEARCH} messages
that dominate one source, a byte-level size edit necessarily corrupts the L7 grammar
even when all IP and UDP fields remain consistent, so such packets cannot be faithfully
realized by overlay at all. They are reported separately rather than scored as
compliance failures, and \Cref{sec:eval-purification} gives the full accounting.
Realization runs off the inference path by design: the protected detector consumes the
purified flow in feature space, while PCAP emission serves validation, forensics, and
any downstream system that requires concrete packets, without adding to the per-packet
latency budget.

Finally, the evaluation harness embeds the \emph{independent} protected detectors
that complement the \ac{TCN}-based purification measurements: a Kitsune instance
(KitNET with the damped incremental statistics its reference implementation calls
AfterImage~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune}) for the two Kitsune-native sources (Mirai and
the SSDP flood), and TANTGen's own ML-NIDS flow classifiers for the eight CIC
attacks, in every case the very detector TANTGen calibrated that attack's evasion
against. The harness realizes matched adversarial, purified, and clean packet
triplets of the same flows and scores each through its attack's detector, so that
purification can be judged by a judge that shares nothing with DEPT's training
loop; the measurement is reported in \Cref{subsec:eval-e2e-detect}.

\section{Implementation and Tools}\label{sec:sol-tools}
DEPT is implemented in Python. The three sequence models (\ac{TCN}, meta-learner,
reconstructor) are built in PyTorch and the \ac{RF} in scikit-learn. The nPrint
encoding is reimplemented natively on top of scapy, following the bit layout of the
original tool~\cite{holland2021nprint}; folding the encoder into the pipeline avoids an
external binary, lets identity masking happen inline, and keeps tensorization a
single pass over each capture. Scapy also drives packet parsing and the realization
step, while \texttt{tshark}, \texttt{editcap}, and \texttt{tcpdump} handle dissection,
time-window filtering of CIC-IDS2017, and the packet-capping of the large CIC-DDoS-2019
captures, respectively.

The system is organized as four model stages behind five configuration files, one per
stage plus one for the pipeline, with the two evaluation studies of
\Cref{chap:evaluation} (the deployment baseline and the gradient ablation) adding one
configuration file each in the same pattern; every file fully determines its stage's
data scope, architecture, and training settings, and a small runner script per stage
executes it end to end. All stages read the same tensor artifacts and the same stored split, which is
how the shared-split discipline of \Cref{sec:sol-pipeline} is enforced in practice,
and derived artifacts are cached and fingerprinted against their upstream inputs (for
instance, the meta-learner's token cache is invalidated whenever a base learner's
checkpoint changes), so any partial re-run stays consistent. \Cref{tab:sol-hyper}
summarizes the learned components. The source layout mirrors the stages, with the
feature pipeline under \texttt{src/feature\_extraction}, the models under
\texttt{src/models}, per-stage entry points under \texttt{scripts}, and configuration
under \texttt{config}; further operational detail lives with the repository rather
than in this document.

With the architecture in place, what remains is to show that it meets its goals: that
the ensemble detects manipulation early, and that purification actually returns
flagged flows to the benign manifold while emitting valid packets. These are the
questions \Cref{chap:evaluation} takes up.
