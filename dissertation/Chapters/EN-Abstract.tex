\noindent Network intrusion detection today relies on machine learning, which brought a
significant weakness signatures didn't have to worry about: the detector itself can be
evaded. An attacker that
reshapes only packet timing and sizes, keeping every packet protocol-compliant, can make
an ongoing attack look benign to a deployed detector. Much of the defensive literature
measures robustness against perturbations no real packet sequence can produce. This
thesis proposes DEPT (Defense via Ensemble and Purification Transformer), a streaming
defense trained and evaluated on realizable adversarial traffic from TANTGen, a
black-box generator built in a sibling thesis. DEPT turns traffic into a masked,
bit-level representation at two temporal granularities; two architecturally different
learners, a Random Forest and a Temporal Convolutional Network, fused per packet by an
attention-based meta-learner, decide whether a flow was manipulated; flagged flows are
corrected by a causal purification Transformer and realized back into sendable packets.
Across ten traffic sources from three public datasets, each manipulated in three
perturbation modes, DEPT gives a verdict from the first packet, where the fusion is the
best model available, and reaches an end-of-flow AUROC of $0.933$; the Random Forest
alone is stronger from the second packet on, and the fusion's benefit is
earliness and a higher cost to a gradient-guided attacker, which never evades the stack
on $74.5\%$ of flows against $45.5\%$ for the sequence model alone. Judged through the
independent detector each attack targeted, purification recovers $63\%$ of the
destroyed detection and cuts attack success from $21.1\%$ to $11.6\%$, with $97.2\%$ of
reconstructed packets parsed cleanly by Wireshark's \texttt{tshark}. The evaluation
reports the failures with equal prominence: on the size-perturbed reflection floods no
model beats chance, and purifying non-evading traffic lowers detection rather than
restoring it.
